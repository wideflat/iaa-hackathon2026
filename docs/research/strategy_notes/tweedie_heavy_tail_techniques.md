## 1. Tweedie power selection and tuning; Tweedie GBM vs frequency-severity vs two-stage hurdle; sensitivity of the metric to the loss

### Takeaway
In M5 the winners used a single LightGBM `tweedie` objective with a low power of 1.1 (not tuned per series, not a hurdle model); I found no top solution that ran a systematic power sweep or reported the metric's sensitivity to it, so the evidence that power choice matters (or does not) is essentially absent. The more consistent evidence is that Tweedie/Poisson beat plain MSE/MAE for sparse targets by modest margins, and that the series-level MLE power varied widely (1.0 to 1.5), which supports "do not over-invest in one global p".

### Cited Findings
- M5 1st place (Yeonjun In) code: `objective: tweedie`, `tweedie_variance_power: 1.1`, `learning_rate 0.015`, `n_estimators 3000`, `num_leaves 2**11-1` (store models) or `2**8-1` (non-recursive store-dept models), `min_data_in_leaf 2**12-1` / `2**8-1`, `subsample 0.5`, `feature_fraction 0.5`, `max_bin 100`, `boost_from_average False`, metric rmse. Write-up says "LGBM (single), objective = tweedie". — [A1 recursive store train code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A1/3.%20code/2.%20train/1-1.%20recursive_store_TRAIN.py); [1st place write-up](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684)
- 1st place tuning rule of thumb: halve the learning rate and double the number of trees (0.03/1500 to 0.015/3000) "often leads to better result, for me"; extra features were not added because "the more features, the bigger std of my cvs". — [1st place thread comments](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684)
- 4th place (monsaraida): LightGBM single model, `objective = tweedie` (power not stated), one model per store per forecast week, no recursive features, no multiplier. — [4th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)
- 5th place (Alan Lahoud) used LightGBM with a Poisson objective per department (7 models), not Tweedie, so top-5 finishes existed with both. — [5th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163916)
- 3rd place (Jeon et al.) trained a modified DeepAR with Tweedie loss and said their Tweedie choice came from the "Why tweedie works?" thread. — [3rd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164374)
- 2nd place (Matthias Anderer) code lists `objective: tweedie`, `tweedie_variance_power 1.1`, lr 0.2, 3600 rounds, but trains with a custom asymmetric squared loss through `fobj` (see section 2); LightGBM's treatment of `fobj` versus `objective` was not tested by me. — [2nd place code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/m5-final-XX.py); [2nd place write-up](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164599)

- 84th place tried a per-product Tweedie rho but could not make it work "due to how LGBM computes the log link"; a forum thread on replicating LightGBM's Tweedie as a custom objective also stalled because custom objectives lose the log link (gradient unstable near zero predictions). — [84th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127); [thread 155334](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/155334)
- LightGBM documentation: `tweedie_variance_power` default 1.5, constraint 1.0 <= p < 2.0, "set this closer to 2 to shift towards a Gamma distribution". — [LightGBM Parameters.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/LightGBM/master/docs/Parameters.rst)
- Rohlik Sales v2 (a Tweedie-like retail sales task, non-Tweedie metric): 1st place "used Tweedie exclusively for all models" and could not tune CatBoost "maybe because I used tweedie objective as opposed to RMSE"; 2nd place found LightGBM Tweedie "much better than the MAE objective but slightly worse than the sqrt + MSE objective" (target sqrt-transformed, L2 loss). — [Rohlik 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563215); [Rohlik 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563117)

No top-5 solution used an explicit hurdle. — [thread 150614](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/150614); [16th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/169085)
- Secondary: the IJF article "Forecasting with trees" is quoted (by a Rohlik commenter) as saying contestants found the Tweedie loss "particularly effective" and that it "is specifically geared towards sparse (zero-inflated) target data". — [quote in Rohlik 1st place comments](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563215) (the paper itself was not opened)
- Literature on the insurance side (SNIPPETS ONLY): Yang-Qian-Zou's TDboost estimates the Tweedie index by profile likelihood and reports that boosted Tweedie still beats the GLM benchmark with an estimated rather than true index; a 2024 zero-inflated Tweedie paper picks p over a grid in (1,2) by log-likelihood; EMTboost and a CatBoost zero-inflated Tweedie paper extend boosting with an explicit zero-mass component. — [arXiv 1508.06378](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1508.06378); [arXiv 2405.14990](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.14990); [arXiv 2406.16206](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.16206)

### Inferences
- With the metric fixed at p=1.5, the scoring p is not a free hyperparameter: M5 winners' use of 1.1 reflects very sparse unit counts, not a recommendation for claims data. The sensible use of power in the training objective is as a robustness knob (lower p behaves more Poisson-like and dampens the heavy tail; higher p approaches Gamma-like weighting of large values). Whether training at 1.5 versus a lower p moves the D2 at 1.5 beyond the roughly +-0.01 noise is untested in any source I read, so it should be treated as a coin flip unless paired-fold tests show otherwise.
- The M5 "alternatives to Tweedie" that actually worked in the top 5 were Poisson (5th), direct squared-error with asymmetric loss (2nd) and sqrt+L2 (Rohlik 2nd), i.e. a monotone transform plus a simple loss can match Tweedie. This weakly supports our frequency x severity design being at par with a Tweedie GBM, as the user already observed.
- Per-series power heterogeneity (MLE 1.0 to 1.5 in M5; our lines motor/buildings/contents are even more different) argues for fitting by line rather than a single pooled p, with the pooled D2 still evaluated at 1.5.

### Gaps
- No source compared Tweedie power values (1.1 vs 1.3 vs 1.5) on a scored holdout. The M5 papers (IJF) and Amazon "Forecasting with trees" were not reachable, so any quantified Tweedie-vs-other gain in those is not captured.
- No competition source found that scores a pooled Tweedie deviance on insurance claims or compares hurdle to Tweedie head to head; insurance literature came as snippets only.

## 2. Level and bias corrections: multipliers/scalers, how estimated, measured gain, per-segment calibration, trend extrapolation to the next period

### Takeaway
In M5 the single largest lever was the overall level of predictions: a global multiplier of about 0.95 (private) versus about 1.03 (public) moved teams by hundreds of ranks, and it was known only via leaderboard probing; the honest, non-probing method used by the 2nd-place team (aligning aggregates to an independent trend model, expressed as an asymmetric-loss multiplier) worked, while data-driven per-item trend multipliers and "history-based" uplifts failed. The transferable lesson is to estimate the level from an independent aggregate/trend model with a stability check across several windows, never from the GBM's own residuals alone and never from one validation window.

### Cited Findings
- Sensitivity: Chris Deotte wrote that a 5% total-level error moves the M5 LB by 0.1 (corrected from an initial "0.01" typo in the thread) and that the multiplier "can move your final leaderboard rank up or down 200+ positions". First place scored about 0.52, 100th about 0.60, 200th about 0.63, 300th about 0.65. — [Shake Up Explained](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163621)
- Level history misled: in all four previous years, sales in the target 28 days exceeded the preceding validation window, so most teams predicted about 1.29M units; probing showed the true figure was about 1.22M (public about 1.232M). — [Shake Up Explained](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163621)
- Giba: best multiplier about 1.03 on public LB and about 0.955 on private; estimated during the competition as `sum(public preds)*1.03 == sum(private preds)*alpha`; his submitted blend used 0.98, and 0.955 would have put him in the gold zone. Others: public kernel with evaluation-period data, seed averaging and x0.95 reached private 0.52838 (equal to 3rd place), x0.945 or x0.955 on strong models would have been top 1-3, x0.936 gave 0.52661, one participant's computed private multiplier was 0.993 and applying it made things slightly worse, another computed 0.969 and chose not to apply. — [Have no idea what's going on](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163047); [Shake Up Explained comments](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163621)
- Multiplier was not stable even across weeks within the public window: one competitor found best multipliers of 1.0, 1.01 and 1.03 for three successive validation weeks, and the best LB used 1.02. — [Model integration and blending](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/151329)
- 2nd place (private 0.52816): two streams, a per-store LightGBM without any lag/rolling target features, plus an N-BEATS ensemble on the aggregate levels 1-5; the level of the bottom model was set by training 15 versions with a custom asymmetric loss (gradient scaled by a multiplier on the under-prediction side; multiplier 0.9 to 1.23 tried) and choosing the version where the mean error between the aggregated bottom forecasts and N-BEATS "flipped" sign (optimum found around 0.93-0.95), then equal-weight averaging five versions with multipliers 0.9, 0.93, 0.95, 0.97, 0.99. Quote: "the alignment with top level N-BEATS predictions is most likely the most important trick". He did not test post-hoc scaling against the loss-based shift ("I think the loss approach will work better ... but did not compare"). — [2nd place write-up](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164599); [documentation PDF](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/Documentation_v01.pdf); [alignment code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/m5-alignandsubmit.py)
- 1st place and 4th place used no post-processing/multiplier at all ("Without post-processing (e.g. magic multiplier)"; 4th: "Not to use post processing, multiplier, and leaks"). — [1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)
- 5th place (Alan Lahoud): a global multiplier of about 1.03 "really improved the result" but he distrusted it, so he built a store x department correction factor matrix from the last validation week (e.g. 0.92 for FOODS_1 in CA_1 meaning actuals were about 92% of predictions). Private score stayed close to public. — [5th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163916)
- Failure case: a per-item yearly trend "magic multiplier" vector estimated from past data looked good in CV and then scored 0.72667 private versus 0.58797 with the post-processing removed (614 places). Lesson stated: "stay clear of postprocessing for non-stationary data, especially if not properly tuned/tested". — [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279)
- Why multipliers were considered: tree models cannot predict outside the range of training targets (Gilles Vandewiele) unless the target is transformed (e.g. divide target by a past average, per a reply; or model deviations from a rolling baseline), which another competitor accepted as the only route to "extrapolation". — [Not a lottery thread](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163190)
- Conservative competitors: team at rank 47 argued that history showed no precedent for the validation-window surge, ensembled diverse conservative models, and applied no factor ("when there is so much uncertainty in trends then be conservative"). 84th place: "Did not use any kind of arbitrary multiplier as none improved my CV". — [M 5.0 or Earthquake 5.0](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163659); [84th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127)
- A pre-competition alternative to multipliers (untested in the thread): fit a trend model first and pass its residuals to the trees with OOF predictions. — [thread 142874](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/142874)
- SNIPPET ONLY (not opened): Anderer and Li's top-down alignment paper describes a bias multiplier lambda applied for hierarchical alignment and says the optimal value was identifiable only after the competition; a 2026 arXiv preprint (2609.27912, unreviewed) reports that a multiplier estimated from a model's own training errors barely changed error while multipliers anchored to a separate, more accurate model helped, attributing that to the 2nd and 5th M5 entries (attribution UNVERIFIED). — [arXiv 2103.08250](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.08250); [arXiv 2609.27912](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2609.27912)
- Rossmann (snippet only): a search summary of arXiv 2009.07701 says the winner adapted XGBoost to time series and applied a ridge-regression trend adjustment because GBDT cannot extrapolate trends; a "0.985 scaling factor" folklore could NOT be verified. — [arXiv 2009.07701](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.07701)
- Insurance side (SNIPPET): Denuit-Charpentier-Trufin note that models fitted by minimising deviance outside canonical-link GLMs (including early-stopped boosting and neural nets) need not reproduce observed totals, and propose an "autocalibration" step, a local GLM on the first-stage prediction, to restore balance at local rather than only portfolio level. — [arXiv 2103.03635](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03635)

### Inferences
- The user's finding (Tweedie-1.5 GBM under-predicting totals by 20-30% in-sample, fixed by rescaling) matches the Denuit et al. mechanism (regularised/early-stopped deviance minimisation without a balance constraint). The cheap analogue of the M5 2nd-place approach is: fit the rescaling by line (and perhaps per segment) on out-of-fold predictions, then treat only the residual forward-looking drift as a separate, independently estimated trend factor.
- M5 evidence says the dangerous part is the forward-looking extrapolation factor (global 1.03 versus 0.955; per-item trend multiplier lost 0.14), not the in-sample calibration factor. Given an extrapolation year, estimating the 2025 level from an independent aggregate model (claims-per-exposure and severity-trend regressions on annual totals, with conservative damping) and then using the GBM only for relative risk ordering is the closest analogue of the winning alignment trick.
- Per-segment factors (5th place's store-department matrix) only had the small support of one validation week and 70 cells; for claims, per-peril/per-line factors are a safer granularity than per-segment, since 90-97% zeros plus an 11.9M outlier make segment totals noisy. A multiplier ensemble across several values (2nd place averaged five multipliers spanning 0.9-0.99 around the optimum) is a hedge against misestimating the level.
- Exact measured gains of the corrections are in WRMSSE units and cannot be converted to D2; what does transfer is the ratio of importance: level error dominated architecture and feature choices in M5.

### Gaps
- No source quantified how a level error translates into Tweedie deviance (D2); this needs a local simulation on our data (not done here).
- Details of how the 2nd-place N-BEATS reference itself was validated for the target period were not found beyond "manual check"; no formal estimate of the standard error of the chosen multiplier.
- The M5 results paper and the Amazon "Forecasting with trees" paper (both could have more numbers on multipliers/direct vs recursive) were unreachable.

## 3. Ensembling and stacking for Tweedie targets: weights, seeds, feature sets, recursive vs direct; typical gains

### Takeaway
Top solutions mostly used equal-weight averages of a handful to a few dozen diverse models (different grouping, direct vs recursive, loss multipliers, feature sets); measured gains where reported were of the order of 1-3% of the score, and the robust finding is that blending recursive with direct/non-recursive models stabilised results more than any single tuning choice.

### Cited Findings
- M5 1st place: equal-weight average of six LightGBM Tweedie predictions: {recursive, non-recursive} x {by store, by store-category, by store-department}; chosen by mean and standard deviation across three time-based CV windows plus the public window, "especially focusing on std". Observations: recursive scored better overall, non-recursive best at CV3, recursive best at public, hence ensembling for robustness; "group" variation was smaller than recursive/non-recursive variation. Non-recursive models avoided lags shorter than 28 days. — [1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [final ensemble code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A1/3.%20code/3.%20predict/3-1.%20Final%20ensemble.py)
- M5 2nd place: equal-weight average of five models differing only in loss multiplier (see section 2) plus aggregate N-BEATS alignment; N-BEATS top level was an ensemble of 9 models (bagging 3 x meta context lengths 3,5,7), trained with sMAPE loss. — [2nd place documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/Documentation_v01.pdf)
- M5 3rd place: 8 trained DeepAR models, top-3 checkpoints each selected by the mean WRMSSE over the past 14 windows, final ensemble 24 dropout + 19 non-dropout models "just a heuristic". — [3rd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164374)
- M5 4th place: single LightGBM, no blending, split into week-specific direct models per store; explicitly a "practical" choice. — [4th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)
- A mid-table (221st) participant averaged three near-identical LightGBM models differing in which validation rows were randomly held out: public 0.59/0.60/0.61, blend 0.59 (so the spread across seeds-like variants was about 0.02 on that scale). — [Top 4% Solution](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163092)
- 7th place tested the seed sensitivity of his CV by rerunning the same model with different seeds and used hyperopt on a store subset: about 24 hours for 40 parameter sets. — [7th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164826)
- Direct vs recursive: Rohlik 1st place reported "a boost of around ~0.4" (on a score of about 17) from 14 separate daily direct models versus one 14-day model, and about 0.2 from adding XGBoost to LightGBM; tuned XGBoost was ">0.5 better" than default and was deliberately made different from the LightGBM "for diversity gain". Rohlik 2nd place went recursive: ensembling 6-7 direct notebooks gave about 18.2, recursion with one two-model notebook 18.1, cleaned to 17.95; final five-model ensemble 17.385 private versus best single 17.794 (RealMLP) and 17.814-17.836 (XGBoost/LightGBM). — [Rohlik 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563215); [Rohlik 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563117)
- Direct models avoid propagating bias to later horizons at the cost of variance (aagapi, citing Ben Taieb and Hyndman); his own multi-horizon setup was one model per horizon day. — [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279)
- Allstate Claims Severity (MAE metric, heavy-tailed severities): 1st place stacked 81 first-level models (XGBoost with interactions, 10x10-fold bagged NNs, RGF, LightGBM, VW, libFM/FFM and others) into an XGBoost and two NNs, then a Nelder-Mead-optimised weighted blend; best single XGBoost 1105.12 public, best level-2 model 1097.87 public; used a fair-loss objective and `(1+loss)^0.25` and `log(loss+shift)` target transforms, and trained models on percentile subsets of the target to better estimate the exorbitant losses. — [Allstate 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/allstate-claims-severity/discussion/26416)
- Rossmann 1st place blended models by harmonic mean chosen by pairwise selection and greedy forward selection on stored holdout predictions; the harmonic mean "did improve the ensemble a little bit". — [Rossmann 1st place thread](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rossmann-store-sales/discussion/18024)
- Another competitor reported that merging XGBoost into LightGBM made his score 0.004 worse in M5, and that hyperparameter variants lived within the noise of the CV (several M5 participants noted wide CV variance across windows). — [Model integration and blending](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/151329); [4th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)

### Inferences
- A reasonable M5-style recipe for us: equal-weight average (in the prediction domain, since Tweedie output is a mean) of several structurally different learners (frequency x severity GBM, Tweedie GBM after level scaling, GLM), possibly each bagged over seeds; weights from a Nelder-Mead or non-negative least squares fit on pooled out-of-fold deviance are a small further step with an over-fitting risk when positives are only 3-10% of rows.
- Recursive/direct has no analogue in a single-year cross-section, but the "diversity from different groupings and horizons" idea maps onto per-line vs pooled models and different feature sets.
- Gains of blending in the sources (roughly 1-3% of score, e.g. Rohlik 17.794 to 17.385 is 2.3%; Allstate 1105 to 1098 is 0.65%) are comparable to the gains we cannot detect in validation (+-0.01 on D2), so an ensemble is the one move whose benefit is theoretically assured even when it cannot be measured.

### Gaps
- No source reports an ensemble with explicit Tweedie-deviance scoring or optimised stacking weights for a Tweedie target; Allstate weights were for MAE.
- Exact ensemble-gain numbers for M5 (WRMSSE before/after blending) were not found.

## 4. Handling extreme losses: capping/winsorising, excess loads, robust validation

### Takeaway
The competition sources contain almost no evidence on capping because M5 and Rohlik have bounded counts; the nearest evidence is from Allstate (compress the target with power/log transforms and a fair-type loss) and Rohlik (outlier removal did not help), plus several top-solution warnings that CV across time windows is very noisy and must be built around multiple windows and their standard deviation.

### Cited Findings
- Rohlik 1st place: "Outlier removal - removing outliers including outlier time periods such as covid or periods early in a warehouse's history just didn't seem to help." — [Rohlik 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563215)
- Allstate 1st place: target transforms `(1+loss)^0.25` and `log(loss+SHIFT)`, a fair-loss objective with constant 0.7, and additional XGBoost models trained on the bottom 5/10 and top 70/80/90 percentiles of the target "to get a better estimate of the exorbitant loss values". — [Allstate 1st place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/allstate-claims-severity/discussion/26416)
- Rohlik 2nd place: modelled `sqrt(sales - base_estimate)` with L2 loss rather than raw Tweedie, and found sqrt+MSE slightly better than Tweedie and Tweedie much better than MAE. — [Rohlik 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563117)
- M5 top teams concentrated on validation robustness rather than outliers: 1st place used three back-to-back 28-day CV windows plus the public window and chose by mean and std; 3rd place scored 14 past windows because "WRMSSE is severely fluctuated according to the period"; 4th place could not "build a proper validation" because scores varied across time; 84th used six time-series folds with early stopping on the competition metric; the aagapi solution backtested on seven 28-day windows including the same calendar window in earlier years. — [1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [3rd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164374); [4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216); [84th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127); [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279)
- Cleaning out-of-stock zeros (a structural-zero analogue): imputing past stock-out gaps "actually hurt CV scores" and removing them helped only moderately for aagapi; the 84th-place solution flagged stock-out zeros, trained with the flag and corrected the bias in post-processing, reporting a "0.15 boost in CV" (units as stated by the author, unverified) and a 0.03 CV boost from zeroing predictions under 0.1 and rounding 0.9-1 up to 1. — [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279); [84th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127)
- Selection via the exact metric: 84th place used an early-stopping criterion based on the competition metric and weights equal to the metric's level-12 weights, and the 2nd place alignment compared RMSSE and mean error against reference forecasts. — [84th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127)

### Inferences
- None of the cited solutions had a single observation like the 11.9M claim, so there is no direct precedent for severity capping; the Allstate practice (power/log transforms, splitting the target into percentile regimes) and the user's own "capped Gamma severity" are consistent in spirit. For a Tweedie D2 metric, one huge observation dominates the pooled deviance of the evaluation year regardless of the model, so what matters is (a) the baseline level (expected mean including the tail) not being distorted by training on it and (b) an explicit excess/large-loss load added at line level; this is my reasoning, not something a source established.
- Robust validation advice that all of the M5 write-ups share: use several time windows, report mean and std across windows, and judge differences in paired form; the user's observation that changes move validation by less than the noise is exactly the situation that led 1st/3rd/4th place to prefer simple, ensemble-based, low-variance choices.

### Gaps
- No source reports winsorisation levels or excess-load factors for Tweedie metrics; insurance-specific competition write-ups with large-loss capping could not be located.

## 5. Feature engineering patterns that gave real gains and which transfer to policy-level claims

### Takeaway
Time-series lag and rolling-mean features dominated public M5 notebooks, but the 2nd-place team explicitly dropped them as noise for intermittent data and the 4th-place team avoided recursive features, while target/mean encodings, relative-price features, calendar proximity features and exposure-style ratios were the ones reported as materially helpful; for policy-level claims only the encoding/ratio ideas carry over, along with customer-level history as the analogue of lags.

### Cited Findings
- 2nd place: no lag/rolling target features because for intermittent data "there is mostly noise in those signals"; used only datetime, price, SNAP and release features from a public kernel; avoiding history features also avoided unstable cross-validation. — [2nd place documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/Documentation_v01.pdf)
- Counterpoint: 5th place used lag_7, lag_28 and rolling means (7/28 windows at lags 7/28) plus item/store categoricals; 1st place used lag and rolling mean/std features in both recursive and non-recursive form; 7th place used 7/14/28 rolling means but no rolling std. — [5th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163916); [1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [7th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164826)
- Exponentially weighted running means instead of plain rolling means gave "a consistent boost to all my models of at least 0.007 LB (0.011 in the best case)" (alpha 0.05 for the 28-day mean, 0.1 for the 7-day mean). — [Share your Magic](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/147090)
- aagapi: categorical combinations were target-encoded without smoothing, only for high-cardinality variables, with item|store the most successful; price momentum and store/department ids were among the top-5 features; seasonal lags at several periodicities and rolling statistics (avg, median, std, skew, kurtosis, quantiles) were used. — [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279)
- 5th place price features: replaced current price with percentage differences from previous weeks' prices; calendar features: per-event proximity strength limited to 30 days. 84th place: forward/backward event lags (3 days) gave "significant boost in CV (0.05)"; mean encoding on item/department was discarded as no improvement; store-wise models discarded. — [5th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163916); [84th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163127)
- Rohlik 1st place: the features that gave a "significant boost" and were not in public notebooks: mean encoding of sales over orders/weekdays and other attributes, item price/discount relative to its category, and competing-product availability; hyperparameter tuning (optuna plus manual) "gave a considerable gain over defaults". Rohlik 2nd: price- and order-scaled estimates of past sales (an exposure/price-normalised baseline) as base value, sqrt target, and "salt noise" (randomly nulling 5% of lag features) gave only a small gain and was dropped. — [Rohlik 1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563215); [Rohlik 2nd](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/rohlik-sales-forecasting-challenge-v2/discussion/563117)
- Stability over breadth: 1st place declined to add its extra features because they raised CV std; 7th place pruned features that gave small gains and tested seed sensitivity; one mid-table participant found keeping the day index as a predictor "helped my public score a lot" and a very low learning rate (0.01) "reduced my score quite significantly" (unexplained, anecdotal). — [1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [7th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164826); [Top 4%](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163092)
- Group/segment models: 1st place trained by store, store-category and store-department; 2nd by store ("local buying patterns"); 5th by department; aagapi and others one global model; one participant found state-level splitting worse than store-level, while another found a single global model reached 0.460 (fast single-model scores listed in the community table were 0.457-0.47). — [Model integration and blending](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/151329); [Share your Magic](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/147090)

### Inferences
- Transferable to policy-level claims: (a) ratios to a peer-group norm (the relative-price idea) such as premium or sum insured relative to postcode/segment, (b) smoothed target/mean encodings of high-cardinality keys (postcode, broker, vehicle make) built out-of-fold, (c) customer-level history (prior claim count, years since last claim, previous-year claims in other lines) as the analogue of lag features, taken from earlier years only so the 2025 scoring set is consistently constructed, (d) exponentially weighted history rather than flat windows, (e) a price/exposure-normalised baseline with the model fitted on the residual or on a sqrt-scale (Rohlik 2nd).
- Calendar, event and SNAP features are irrelevant, but time-since-event features (days since claim/renewal, policy age) are the cross-sectional analogue.
- Given 90-97% zeros, the 2nd-place argument that history features are noise at the row level applies most strongly to claim counts of a single policy; aggregated history (customer, address, segment) is less noisy.

### Gaps
- The sources are retail demand; no policy-level claim competition write-up was found, so transferability is inferred. Gain sizes are in different metrics (WRMSSE, LB points, CV points) and are quoted only as stated by the authors.

## 6. Which tricks gave gains larger than the noise, and which were noise chasing

### Takeaway
Across M5 and Rohlik the consistently larger-than-noise levers were (1) the overall prediction level, (2) a sound time-structured CV (and aggregating several windows), (3) structurally different models blended with equal weights, (4) direct horizon-specific models or ensembling direct and recursive, and (5) a few domain features; the noise-chasing items were fine hyperparameter tuning, single-window CV selection, and individual seed/variant choices.

### Cited Findings
- Larger than noise (author-reported): total-level error of 5% shifts the M5 score by about 0.1 and ranks by 200+ (sect. 2); 14 direct daily models over one multi-day model gave about 0.4 in Rohlik (on about 17); XGBoost added to LightGBM about 0.2; model ensemble 17.794 to 17.385 (Rohlik 2nd); forward/backward event lags 0.05 CV and stock-out handling (84th, units unverified); EWM rolling means 0.007-0.011 LB; Tweedie over MSE 0.004 CV (one participant); direct vs recursive switch gave 18.2 to 17.95 for Rohlik 2nd. — sources in sections 2, 3 and 5
- Noise or fragile: 1st-place CV std "large" for both recursive and non-recursive baselines, which is why selection used the mean and std over four windows; 4th place gave up on a precise validation; M5's public leaderboard window disagreed in sign with the private window on the best multiplier (1.03 versus 0.955); a per-item trend factor that "worked well in my CV" lost 0.14 on the private score; keeping extra features raised CV variance. — [1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684); [4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216); [aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279); [Have no idea what's going on](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163047)
- 2nd place's own statement of what mattered: do not use history features, align to an independent aggregate forecast; he also notes it will be interesting how solutions do outside the evaluation window. 4th place: a deliberately simple, single-model approach achieved rank 4, and "The ranking itself means absolutely nothing to me". — [2nd place documentation](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Mcompetitions/M5-methods/master/Code%20of%20Winning%20Methods/A2/Documentation_v01.pdf); [4th place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)
- Community consensus statements: "The competition was about choosing the right prediction multiplier" (Giba) versus "if you have so much uncertainty, be conservative / dampen" (rank-47 team) versus "the validation month was an outlier" (miguel perez, his hypothesis); the first and the last disagree on whether an in-window uplift was ever justified, i.e. the explanation of the shake-up remained contested. — [thread 163047](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163047); [thread 163190](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163190); [thread 163659](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163659)
- Hard facts for the "extrapolation year": all M5 solutions predicted the period after a labelled validation window, and top solutions were scored on a single 28-day window, so M5 rankings themselves contain a large luck component by the participants' own assessment ("a lottery" vs "not a lottery" debate). — [thread 163190](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163190)

### Inferences
- Applied to our situation: with validation noise of about +-0.01 unpaired, the only items from this evidence base that are likely above noise are (i) the level (calibrated by line using out-of-fold predictions and an independent trend estimate), (ii) averaging structurally different models, (iii) adding customer/area history aggregates and relative-to-peer ratios, (iv) a paired-fold design (the same folds for all candidates) so small differences become measurable. Further GBM hyperparameter or feature micro-tuning is the part the M5 winners also explicitly avoided.
- The M5 shake-up warns that choosing the final extrapolation factor using a single holdout year is the highest-variance decision; the 2nd-place method of averaging several scale variants centred on the independent estimate is the cheapest protection.

### Gaps
- No source provides paired-difference standard errors or a significance test for any trick; "larger than noise" is the authors' own judgement or a leaderboard difference, not a statistical test.
- Insurance-specific Kaggle/AICrowd pricing-game write-ups with level-calibration numbers were not found.

## 1. Data bases: calendar year, accident year, policy year, report year; exposure/loss matching; "calendar-accident year"; does CY incurred include prior-AY development?

### Takeaway
Ratemaking is most often done on an accident year (AY) basis (or policy year, PY, where available) because AY/PY losses can be matched to the exposure that generated them; calendar year (CY) incurred mixes in reserve changes on prior accident years and so is distorted by reserving inaccuracy. Yes, CY incurred includes development on prior accident years; AY incurred does not. Report year is used mainly for claims-made coverages. I found no source defining "calendar-accident year" as a standalone term beyond a CAS paper title.

### Cited Findings
- Accident year is "the usual basis for ratemaking": loss dollars are assigned to the year the accident happened regardless of when the claim was reported or paid (Exam 5 study-guide summaries of Werner & Modlin). (snippet) — [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt (RisingFellow, 2024)](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf) and [ACTEX CAS5 formula sheet](https://www.actexlearning.com/formula-sheets/CAS5_FS.pdf) (both only seen via search summary)
- Policy year: losses are allocated to the year in which the policy was written. It gives the best loss-to-premium match, but data are not available promptly (a policy year spans two calendar years of accidents with annual policies) and it is more costly because a separate system/data extract must be maintained. (snippet) — [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf)
- Calendar year: losses are allocated to the year in which payments were made and reserves were changed. Available quickly but inaccurate for estimating incurred losses because of distortions from reserving inaccuracies. (snippet) — [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf)
- Report year: losses grouped by when the claim was reported rather than when it occurred; typically used for claims-made policies; described as similar to calendar-accident aggregation except it keys on report date. (snippet) — [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf)
- University ratemaking handout (Thompson, UNI): policy year is "the only method that explicitly matches losses, premiums and exposure units to a set group of insureds" but takes longer to gather; accident-year incurred loss = payments plus reserves on accidents occurring in that year, and premium/loss transactions generally match with a slight time lag; calendar-year losses = paid during the year regardless of when they occurred plus change in reserves for all years (current and prior); calendar year is the least accurate for matching since accounting records lack exposure data. Exposures can be measured written, earned or in-force; earned exposures are those actually exposed to loss during the period. (snippet) — [Ratemaking Methods in Insurance Operations Part 2 (UNI)](https://sites.uni.edu/thompsona/Ratemaking%20Methods%20in%20Insurance%20Operations%20%20Part%202.pdf)
- Accident-year experience "isolates the underwriting performance of a single accident year by excluding reserve development from prior accident years"; calendar-year experience "reflects all losses and reserve changes recognized during a particular accounting year, including development from prior accident years." AY incurred losses for the year still move as claims settle while the AY earned premium is fixed. (snippet; trade glossary, low-to-moderate authority) — [Captive.com glossary: Accident Year Experience](https://www.captive.com/news/glossary-spotlight-accident-year-experience); [Captive.com glossary: Calendar Year Experience](https://www.captive.com/news/glossary-spotlight-calendar-year-experience)
- A CAS paper (Proceedings) describes accident year as matching premiums with losses and expenses arising from the portions of policies in effect during a 12-month period; calendar year "reflects the past profitability of a company including inaccuracies in reserves established in earlier years"; indicated rate changes do not change past profitability but anticipate future profitability. (snippet) — [CAS Proceedings, vol. 79 (proceed79_79038)](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed79_79038.pdf)
- A CAS Proceedings paper titled "The Advantages of Calendar-Accident Year Experience" exists (vol. 58); "calendar-accident year" appears there as a hybrid concept, but I only saw the title/URL in search results, not its content. — [Proceedings 58, p. 20](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed58_58020.pdf). A related CAS abstract titled "advantages calendar accident year experience and need appropriate trend and projection" also exists. — [CAS abstract](https://casact.org/abstract/advantages-calendar-accident-year-experience-and-need-appropriate-trend-and-projection)
- CAS Exam 5 syllabus organizes ratemaking data around calendar year, policy year and accident year (the hybrid term is not a standard syllabus category). (snippet) — [CAS Exam 5 syllabus](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2021-11/Exam5_Syllabus.pdf)
- The Werner & Modlin text (p. ~113 per a CAS E-Forum citation) discusses how changes in exposure distort calendar-year trend; accident-year trend analysis is therefore often preferred. (snippet) — [Medina, CAS E-Forum 2011](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf)
- The CAS exam syllabus has historically addressed whether calendar-year data may be used to determine trends applied to accident-year data; papers choose either CY or AY data and linear or exponential regression "with little guidance regarding which is more appropriate." (snippet) — [CAS abstract / paper on calendar-accident year and trend](https://casact.org/abstract/advantages-calendar-accident-year-experience-and-need-appropriate-trend-and-projection)
- In a CAS E-Forum example, trend estimated from calendar-year data was overstated when development factors were constant across accident years. (snippet) — [Medina, CAS E-Forum 2011](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf)

### Inferences
- A dataset where each row has an exposure period (e.g., a policy-term or vehicle-year record) and a loss is attached only if its loss date falls inside that row's exposure period is, in substance, an accident-year-style / "exposure-period matched" basis: the loss is matched to the exposure that was actually in force on the accident date. Aggregating such rows by the calendar period of the exposure (earned exposure and losses occurring in it) = AY; aggregating by the policy's inception year with all of its losses (even those arising in the following calendar year) = PY. This mapping is my inference from the definitions above, not an explicit statement of any source.
- "Calendar-accident year" (CAS Proceedings title) most plausibly means calendar-year earned exposure/premium combined with losses by accident date (i.e., AY losses vs CY earned premium), the only version of "calendar" that avoids prior-year reserve development; unverified because I could not read the paper.
- For forecasting ultimate cost per exposure: use AY (or exposure-period-matched) ultimates, never CY incurred, as the target; CY incurred contains reserve releases/strengthening on old accidents that bear no relation to the exposure in the denominator.
- Implementation consequence: with loss-date-in-exposure-period matching, only claims that have occurred and been reported by the evaluation date are observed; IBNR/IBNER development to ultimate (section 2) is mandatory for recent periods.

## 2. Pure premium and the loss-projection steps (development to ultimate, trend, large-loss and catastrophe adjustments, LAE, projection to the rating period)

### Takeaway
Pure premium (loss cost) = losses / exposure = frequency x severity. The Werner & Modlin-style projection takes AY (or PY) reported/paid losses (often with ALAE), develops them to ultimate, adjusts for large losses and catastrophes, adds LAE as appropriate, trends them from the experience period to the future rating period, and divides by (earned) exposure. I could only confirm the individual pieces from snippets; the exact ordering was not directly verifiable.

### Cited Findings
- CAS syllabus: pure premium P = L / E (P = pure premium, L = losses, E = exposure units). (snippet) — [McClenahan, "Ratemaking" (CAS syllabus ch. 3)](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2021-10/syllabus_ch3.pdf)
- Werner & Modlin (via excerpt of 5th ed. pp. 94-109): severity = average loss per claim; pure premium = average loss per exposure = frequency x severity; the numerator and denominator choices must be consistent across the frequency and severity calculations. (snippet) — [Basic Ratemaking 5th ed. excerpt (pp. 94-109)](https://consumerwatchdog.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/IRH-SFG-163-2016-05-01-Basic-Ratemaking-Fifth-Edition-Pages-94-109-Excerpted.pdf)
- Ultimate losses = reported losses + IBNR + IBNER (IBNER = development on known claims). (snippet) — [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt / CAS Basic Ratemaking summaries](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf)
- Large loss: one simple method caps individual reported losses at a threshold (example $1,000,000, giving "non-excess losses"), then applies the long-run ratio of excess to capped losses as an excess-loss provision; the cap can be indexed for trend and applied to untrended ground-up losses. (snippet) — [Stolyarov Old Exam 5 guide](https://rationalargumentator.com/actuaryguide/Stolyarov_Old_5_Study_Guide.pdf) and [Exam 5 Study Guide excerpt](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf)
- Catastrophes are treated separately from individual shock losses (events that are unusually severe and produce a significant number of claims, e.g., hurricanes, earthquakes). (snippet) — same Exam 5 guide.
- LAE: ALAE is tied to specific claims (e.g., outside counsel) and is often included with loss; ULAE is overhead not assignable to a claim, commonly loaded as a ratio to incurred loss plus ALAE. (snippet) — same Exam 5 guide.
- Canadian rate filings describe trend as two adjustments: past trend (experience period to historical cost level) and future trend (for changes expected while new premiums are in effect); "loss trend rates are applied to the experience period incurred losses to adjust for the cost levels anticipated during the policy period covered under the proposed rate program." (snippet) — [Oliver Wyman trend report, Nova Scotia (NSERBT, Dec 2015)](https://nserbt.ca/sites/default/files/OW_Trend_Report_%28Signed%29_-_Comm_-_Dec_2015_Data.pdf)
- A CAS paper on trend and loss development factors works a 30-month trend example: AY1969 experience (average accident date July 1, 1969) for a rate revision effective January 1, 1971 -> 30 months from the experience average accident date to the average accident date of policies being rated (effective-date lag plus half of the policy term plus the experience-period offset), i.e., 30 months. (snippet) — [Trend and Loss Development Factors, CAS Proceedings 70](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed70_70001.pdf)
- Study-guide Mahler sample: for 12-month policies written 1/1/08-12/31/08 the average written date is 7/1/08, so the average accident date is 6 months later, 1/1/09; the trend period runs from that to the average accident date for the proposed policies. (snippet) — [Mahler's Guide to Basic Ratemaking sample](https://www.actuarialbookstore.com/samples/Mahler%205C-MAH-12SSM-E%20sample%202-6-12.pdf)

### Inferences (worked formulas; not from a source unless stated)
- Generic projection for experience period i:
  `Ultimate_i = Reported_i (or Paid_i) x CDF_i (to ultimate) [+ IBNR counts/IBNER already inside CDF]`
  `Projected loss cost = sum_i [ (Ultimate_i adj. for large loss / cat) x LAE load x TrendFactor_i ] / sum_i EarnedExposure_i`
  where TrendFactor_i = (1 + annual trend)^(years from avg accident date of period i to avg accident date of the future period).
- Order matters: develop first (development factors are estimated on untrended triangles), then trend. Cap large losses at a threshold that is itself deflated/indexed to each year's cost level; cat losses are removed and a long-term cat load is added back separately.
- Frequency x severity route: `Ultimate frequency_i = Ultimate claim count_i / Earned exposure_i`; `Ultimate severity_i = Ultimate loss_i / Ultimate count_i`; trend each separately and multiply for the projected pure premium (see section 3).
- Numerical example (my own, for implementation testing): AY exposure 10,000 car-years, ultimate loss $6.0M -> pure premium $600. If annual trend is +5% and the trend period is 2.5 years: 600 x 1.05^2.5 = 600 x 1.1297 = $677.8. For a 30-month period, the unsourced arithmetic 1.05^2.5 ~ 1.130 was also computed in a search summary (marked there as the model's own calculation).
- If the target is "expected ultimate cost per exposure for a future period", the forecast = weighted average (by earned exposure, or by credibility) of the trended, developed pure premiums of the experience years, or equivalently a fitted trend line evaluated at the future average accident date.

## 3. Loss trend: frequency, severity, trend period, fit form, number of points, application, magnitude

### Takeaway
Trend is fitted to AY (exposure-adjusted) frequency, severity and/or pure premium series, usually with an exponential (log-linear) least-squares fit giving a constant annual percentage change; it is applied from the average accident date of each experience period to the average accident date of the future period. Separate frequency and severity trends are preferred when the components move differently, but pure premium trend is also examined. I could not retrieve Werner & Modlin's specific guidance on number of points or the linear vs exponential choice.

### Cited Findings
- Exponential model: log(y_t) = b0 + b1 t, estimated by OLS on logged values; annual trend factor = exp(b1); projected value at target time T* = exp(b0 + b1 T*). Exponential yields constant annual % change; linear yields constant $ change per year. A higher R-squared is generally preferred; linear can produce negative values for frequency/severity, so is inappropriate for non-negative quantities. (snippet) — [Medina, CAS E-Forum 2011](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf); [Dickmann & ..., "Considerations in Estimating Loss Cost Trends" (CAS E-Forum 2001)](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_01wforum_01wf021.pdf); [McClenahan, Principles of Ratemaking](https://forum.casact.org/article/168363-chapter-1-principles-of-ratemaking.pdf)
- Usual AY frequency workflow: develop claim counts to ultimate, compute ultimate frequency per accident year, then fit a linear or exponential regression (regress log of frequency on a time index). (snippet) — [Medina, CAS E-Forum 2011](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf)
- Exponential trend factor applied: (1 + trend)^(trend period). (snippet) — [Basic Ratemaking slide deck (airc.org.tw)](https://www.airc.org.tw/newsfiles/BasicRatemakingI.pdf)
- Trend-period convention (12-month policies): average accident date of experience policies = average written date + 6 months; trend runs from there to the average accident date of the proposed policies, which depends on the rate effective date, the length of time the rates will be in force, and the policy term. (snippet) — [Mahler sample](https://www.actuarialbookstore.com/samples/Mahler%205C-MAH-12SSM-E%20sample%202-6-12.pdf); [CAS Proceedings 70](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed70_70001.pdf)
- For AY experience the average accident date is the midpoint of the AY (e.g., AY1969 -> July 1, 1969); 30-month trend period to the future accident date in the CAS example. (snippet) — [CAS Proceedings 70](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed70_70001.pdf)
- Werner & Modlin: pure premium trend reflects changes in both frequency and severity; Modlin's GLM slides note severity trends can mask frequency signal, a reason to examine components separately. (snippet) — [Modlin, CAS GLM II seminar slides (2008)](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/specsem_f2008_handouts_modlin.pdf)
- Worked example in a regulatory/Oliver Wyman trend report: exponential fits over trailing 12-month periods ending each quarter, fitted annual changes shown for 4, 6, 8, 12, 16 and 20 data points, separately for frequency, severity and pure premium; selections are then judgmental. (snippet) — [Oliver Wyman trend report, Nova Scotia](https://nserbt.ca/sites/default/files/OW_Trend_Report_%28Signed%29_-_Comm_-_Dec_2015_Data.pdf)
- Secondary guide (not Werner & Modlin): trend factors applied from the midpoint of the experience period to the future policy period; trend frequency and severity separately when they move at different rates. — [MetricGate loss cost trending](https://metricgate.com/docs/loss-cost-trending/) (low authority; snippet)
- Trend magnitude, recent US auto (illustrative only, mixed sources): motor vehicle insurance CPI +6.4% y/y (April 2025) vs +22.6% (April 2024); one insurer's personal auto incurred severity slowed to 1% in 1H2024 from 11% a year earlier; total-loss frequency rising (22.6% of losses through April 2025). — [Swiss Re US motor](https://www.swissre.com/reinsurance/insights/global-motor-reinsurance-perspectives/us.html); [CCC Crash Course 2025](https://www.cccis.com/reports/crash-course-2025/q2). Social inflation: an III analysis of a CAS paper estimates increasing inflation raised personal auto liability losses by $91.6-$102.3 billion, 8.7%-9.7% of booked loss and DCC. — [CAS/III Increasing Inflation Liability Paper (2025)](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2025-10/2025-III-Increasing-Inflation-Liability-Paper.pdf)
- BattleActs forum: for a Fall 2014 problem, trending from 2012 over 3 and 4 years and averaging gave 327.98, very close to the sample answer that trended from 2013; shows sensitivity to the trend-start convention is small but non-zero. (snippet) — [BattleActs discussion, Fall 2014 Q5](https://battleacts.discourse.group/t/fall-2014-q5-cat-portion/4356)

### Inferences (worked formulas; not from a source)
- Fit form: ln(PP_t) = a + b t over AY t = 1..n; annual trend = e^b - 1; weight by exposure (WLS with weights = earned exposure or claim counts for severity) to avoid thin-year leverage. Standard error of b and R-squared aid selection; exponential preferred for positive quantities, linear can be sanity check.
- Trended ultimate for AY i to future period F: `Ult_i x exp(b x (t_F - t_i))`, where t_i = midpoint of AY i (or average accident date of the exposure period), t_F = average accident date of the future exposure period. Example (mine): experience = AY ending 12/31/2024 (midpoint 7/1/2024); future policies effective 7/1/2027 with 12-month terms -> average accident date 1/1/2028 -> trend period 3.5 years; with +4% annual frequency and +6% severity, pure-premium trend ~ 1.04 x 1.06 = 1.1024 per year, factor 1.1024^3.5 = 1.407.
- Frequency/severity separately vs pure premium: separating avoids confounding from mix changes (e.g., more small claims lower severity while frequency rises); the product of separately selected trends should be compared with the direct pure premium trend as a reasonableness check.
- Calendar-year-based trend risks the distortions flagged in the E-Forum example (exposure growth, development); fit on AY ultimates with consistent development instead.
- Number of points: more points lower variance but include stale regimes (e.g., 2020-2022 COVID, repair-cost inflation); practice is to compare several windows (as in the 4-20 point table) and select judgmentally, possibly using external indices (CPI components, parts/labor, medical, wage) for severity.

- No primary text retrieved for Werner & Modlin's guidance on (a) minimum number of data points, (b) when to choose linear vs exponential, (c) formal treatment of mix change and social inflation.

- The metricgate pure premium page quoted a shortcut (1+tau)^(n - i + 1.5); I treat it as unverified and did not rely on it.

## 4. Exposure considerations: earned exposure, partial-period and mid-term cancellations, mid-term adjustments, exposure-weighted analysis

### Takeaway
The denominator should be earned exposure (exposure units actually exposed to loss in the period), not written or in-force counts; partial-period exposure (e.g., a fraction of a year) must be reflected, and mid-term cancellations/endorsements change both exposure and risk composition. Weighting analyses by earned exposure is standard.

### Cited Findings
- Exposures can be measured written, earned or in-force; earned exposures are the units actually exposed to loss during the period. (snippet) — [UNI ratemaking handout](https://sites.uni.edu/thompsona/Ratemaking%20Methods%20in%20Insurance%20Operations%20%20Part%202.pdf)
- Policy-year/accident-year exposure aggregation by calendar year, policy year and in-force is covered in Infinite Actuary sample material; only the aggregation framing was visible. (snippet) — [Infinite Actuary sample](https://theinfiniteactuary.com/pages/download_sample.php?lid=16450)
- Risk exposure for a contract is the fraction of the year it was active (between 0 and 1); two weighting approaches for mid-term cancellations in Tweedie models are offset and ratio-weighted regressions. (snippet) — [Comparison of Offset and Ratio Weighted Regressions in Tweedie Models (arXiv 2502.11788)](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.11788v1)
- Boucher, Coulibaly and Trufin (April 2026 arXiv): standard ratemaking treats exposure as a mechanical scaling factor; mid-term cancelers show higher claim frequency and severity; cancellation penalties can reallocate roughly 11% of premium; cancellations (termination before scheduled end) differ from vehicle additions/replacements, which keep the original policy end date. (snippet) — [arXiv 2604.02400](https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.02400)
- Werner & Modlin's cited concern: changes in exposure distort calendar-year trend analysis (p. ~113). — [Medina, CAS E-Forum 2011](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf) (snippet)

### Inferences
- With rows carrying an exposure period [start, end] and a loss date, earned exposure of a row within reporting window W is `max(0, min(end, W_end) - max(start, W_start)) / 365` car-years; a loss counts toward W only if its loss date falls inside both the row's exposure period and W. Splitting policies at endorsement dates (a new row per change) preserves the correct exposure-to-risk match.
- Cancelled-early rows contribute only their earned fraction; ignoring this (counting a full year) understates pure premium. Because early cancellers can be riskier (per the arXiv paper), their fractional rows should still be included, not dropped.
- Aggregate to period by summing earned exposure and summing losses (ratio of sums), not by averaging row-level ratios; fit trend lines with exposure weights.
- Write-year vs earned: for a forecast of cost per exposure, express the future-period denominator as earned exposure with the same convention, otherwise growth in new business distorts comparisons.

### Gaps
- Werner & Modlin's exposure chapter (ch. 5: written/earned/in-force exposure, pro-rata earning, on-leveling) was not retrieved; no cited formula for earned exposure computation, only definitions.
- Little source material on mid-term endorsement treatment for loss cost studies beyond the cancellation literature.

## 5. Testing the adequacy of development and trend assumptions (backtesting)

### Takeaway
Actuaries test development assumptions via actual-vs-expected comparisons of emergence against the prior study's factors and by retrospective tests on mature years; the same logic (project from an earlier evaluation, compare with later actual) can be applied to trend. Source coverage for ratemaking-specific backtests of the combined development-plus-trend pure premium was thin.

### Cited Findings
- Actual vs expected test (CAS reserving handout): apply interpolated prior-study cumulative development factors to prior paid/incurred losses to get expected current values, compare with actual; if actual activity is lower (higher) than expected, select current factors producing lower (higher) ultimates than the prior study; for the oldest AY, extrapolate the CDF from the decay pattern. (snippet) — [CLRS 2013 handout](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/clrs_2013_handouts_paper_2307_handout_1293_0.pdf); [CLRS 2014 handout](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/clrs_2014_handouts_paper_3457_handout_2221_0.pdf)
- A CAS E-Forum paper reviews selected development factors against data-indicated factors without judgment overlay, and decomposes changes between prior and current ultimates into data, assumptions and judgment. (snippet) — [CAS E-Forum Fall 2013 (Balester/Kirschner)](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_13fforum_04-balester-kirschner.pdf)
- WCIRB retrospective test: compares each accident year's projected developed loss ratio (from paid/incurred development at ages 108-360 months) against actual reported incurred loss ratio at the target age. (snippet) — [WCIRB document 32101](https://www.wcirb.com/document/32101)
- ASOP 53 (effective for work performed on or after August 1, 2018) governs estimating future costs for prospective P&C risk transfer and retention, including ratemaking; I could not retrieve its sections on trend, development or reasonableness. — [ASB ASOP 53 page](https://www.actuarialstandardsboard.org/asops/estimating-future-costs-prospective-propertycasualty-risk-transfer-risk-retention/) (snippet of scope only)

### Inferences (suggested implementation, not sourced)
- Backtest design: for each past "as-of" date d, use only data available at d (valid-at-d triangles), apply the same CDFs, trend selection and large-loss/cat handling, and forecast the ultimate pure premium for accident year d+1, d+2...; compare with the later best-estimate (mature/near-ultimate) pure premium for those years. Report bias (mean of forecast/actual - 1), error dispersion (MAPE, RMSE on log scale), and by-age performance.
- Separate the two error sources: (1) development: compare projected ultimates of a mature accident year at ages 12, 24, 36... against the value at maturity; (2) trend: using fully developed history, forecast a held-out later year and compare.
- Diagnostics: residual pattern by AY, stability of fitted trend across windows, comparison against external severity/frequency indices, and sensitivity of the forecast to +/- 1 point in annual trend and +/- CDF at the youngest age.

### Gaps
- No ratemaking-specific backtesting procedure from Werner & Modlin or ASOP 53 retrieved; the reserving A-vs-E and retrospective tests are the closest sources.
- No quantitative benchmarks (acceptable error or bias) found.

# Fix three immaturity biases before forecasting 2025 cost

**Ultimate claim cost is not one adjustment but a stack of distinct gaps (unreported claims, case-estimate development, recoveries not yet booked, declined claims not yet decided, large-loss noise and cost trend), and a single 31 Dec 2024 snapshot can measure only some of them.** It can measure claim-count completeness (pure IBNR), because report dates let us rebuild a report-lag triangle; our own analysis shows stable link ratios and count uplifts of +0.6% (AY2023) against +7.9% (AY2024) for motor, and +6% against +82% for bodily injury (BI). It cannot measure paid or case-incurred development (IBNER), so that term must be assumed and sensitivity-tested, not estimated. Recoveries turn out to be a small bias once checked against our data (see the correction in the recoveries section): only 28% of closed motor own-damage/theft claims carry a recovery, averaging 77% of incurred when present, so the expected recovery is about 18.5% of incurred, and these perils are only 12% of open 2024 incurred, so unbooked recoveries overstate 2024 motor net cost by roughly 1.5%. We recommend a model-based Bornhuetter-Ferguson (BF) / Cape Cod analogue: a Tweedie model whose offset carries exposure, row-level count completeness and a trend index; a target adjusted for expected recoveries and declines on open claims; a per-claim cap with an explicit excess load; and validation that scores predictions at the maturity the labels actually have. The 0.64-0.75 under-prediction of 2023 should not be blamed on trend until it is re-measured without the 11.9M BI claim, because trend alone would have to be roughly 20-55% a year to explain it. **Evidence caveat:** every external source behind this report was seen only as a search-result summary, because the sandbox blocked the primary texts (Friedland, Werner & Modlin, Mack, Grace, ASOP 39 and others); only the scikit-learn documentation was read in full. Recommendations are therefore tagged below as source-backed (S), source-adapted to our data (A) or the researchers' own inference (I).

## Ultimate cost is a stack of gaps, and a snapshot sees only part of it

Ultimate loss is the total cost of a fully developed set of claims: paid plus outstanding reported amounts plus IBNR ([IRMI](https://www.irmi.com/term/insurance-definitions/ultimate-loss)). Reported (case-incurred) cost is paid plus case reserves on open claims, and the gap to ultimate is the "broad" IBNR, which splits into pure IBNR (occurred, not yet reported) and IBNER (expected development on claims already reported, negative if case reserves are redundant). Sources use the words inconsistently: some reserve "IBNR" for unreported claims only, others add reopened claims and case development ([Huggins Actuarial](https://hugginsactuarial.com/how-to-explain-ibnr/)). We use the narrow split throughout. Triangle methods estimate the broad quantity in one number and cannot separate the two components without separate count and case-reserve histories (I). That limitation is decisive here, because our data contain one valuation date and no case-estimate history.

The standard toolkit orders itself by how much it trusts actual versus expected experience. Chain ladder multiplies reported cost by a cumulative development factor (CDF), the product of selected age-to-age links and a tail; its core assumption is that past patterns continue, and it is distorted when case-reserving practice or claim mix changes ([Wikipedia: chain-ladder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain-ladder_method)). Link ratios are averaged (simple, volume-weighted, latest-n, medial) and then selected by judgment, usually leaning on volume-weighted averages ([MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/docs/loss-development-triangle/)). Tails come from repeating the last link (Bondy), exponential decay fitted to (link minus 1), or Sherman's inverse power curve, with external benchmarks as the fourth route ([Miller, CLRS 2004](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/clrs_2004_handouts_miller.doc); [Sherman tail article](https://variancejournal.org/article/120867-tail-factor-convergence-in-sherman-s-inverse-power-curve-loss-development-factor-model)). The log-linear fit requires every link above 1.000, a practical trap on flat late columns ([chainladder-python tails](https://chainladder-python.readthedocs.io/main/user_guide/tails.html)). The expected-loss method ignores emerging data, BF adds an a priori amount for the unreported share, and Cape Cod estimates the a priori from all years via "used-up premium" ([chainladder-python Ch. 10](https://chainladder-python.readthedocs.io/main/friedland/chapter_10.html); [LossReserves.com](https://lossreserves.com/learn/bornhuetter-ferguson/)). BF is favoured for recent, immature years where one claim can swing a chain-ladder projection, but it is only as good as its a priori and its percent-reported pattern ([ActuaryPath](https://www.actuarypath.com/concepts/bornhuetter-ferguson/)). Berquist-Sherman restates triangles when case adequacy or settlement speed has changed, and needs open and closed counts and average case reserves by age across diagonals ([BattleActs Ch. 13 notes](https://battleactsmain.ca/v2/assets/doc/ReservingChapter13Berquist-ShermanMethod.pdf)).

Frequency and severity develop through different mechanisms, which is the reason to separate them. Chain ladder on a reported-count triangle converts directly into a reporting-delay distribution ([Verrall and Wüthrich](https://www.bayes.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/368837/prediction-of-rbns-and-ibnr-claims.pdf)), whereas severity development depends on case reserving and closure speed; Werner and Modlin require that numerator and denominator of frequency and severity share the same basis ([Basic Ratemaking excerpt](https://consumerwatchdog.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/IRH-SFG-163-2016-05-01-Basic-Ratemaking-Fifth-Edition-Pages-94-109-Excerpted.pdf)). Serious BI claims close later than minor ones, so average closed cost rises with age ([CAS: BI claims-closure model](https://casact.org/abstract/evaluating-bodily-injury-liabilities-using-claims-closure-model)), a reason to distrust any severity read from early-closing claims. The table maps each component to what our snapshot can do.

| Gap between observed and ultimate | Estimable from one 31 Dec 2024 snapshot? | Handling | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure IBNR counts | Yes: report-lag count triangle from loss_date and report_date; project finding of stable links | Count completeness by line, BI separate, applied as a row-level offset | S + A |
| Dollar size of late-reported claims | Partly: severity-by-report-lag in mature years (AY2021-22), at today's valuation | Test whether late claims differ in size; adjust completeness to dollars if so | I |
| IBNER (paid or case development) | No: needs earlier snapshots | Treat case-incurred as unbiased base; run sensitivity; do not fabricate factors | S (requirement) + I |
| Recoveries on open claims | Partly: recovery rate on closed claims of mature years | Replace missing recovery by expected recovery on open own-damage/theft | A + I |
| Declined claims still undecided | Partly: decline share in mature years versus 2024 | Multiply open-claim expectation by survival probability | I |
| Large-loss volatility | Yes, as a cross-section | Per-claim cap plus excess load | S + A |
| Cost trend | Weakly: four accident years | Explicit trend offset, shrunk, plus calibration check | S + I |

The row-to-period mapping matters too. Accident-year (or exposure-period-matched) grouping assigns each loss to the exposure in force on the loss date and excludes reserve changes on earlier years; calendar-year data mix in prior-year development and are distorted by reserve inaccuracy; policy year gives the best loss-to-premium match but is slower to mature; report year suits claims-made cover ([Exam 5 study guide excerpt](https://risingfellow.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/11/Exam5Guide_Excerpt.pdf); [UNI ratemaking handout](https://sites.uni.edu/thompsona/Ratemaking%20Methods%20in%20Insurance%20Operations%20%20Part%202.pdf)). Our rows, which attach a claim when its loss date falls inside the row's exposure period, are accident-year-style by construction (A, my mapping from these definitions), so no calendar-year feature (for example payments made in 2024) belongs in the model. One check is unresolved: if a row covers a full policy term that straddles 31 Dec 2024, only the elapsed part is observed, and earned exposure must be cut at the snapshot (I).

## Count completeness is the one development pattern the snapshot can measure

Completeness at lag k is the probability a claim is reported by then, and it equals 1/CDF on a count triangle; the reporting-delay distribution is its increments (I; consistent with the chain-ladder-to-delay conversion in [Verrall and Wüthrich](https://www.bayes.citystgeorges.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0008/368837/prediction-of-rbns-and-ibnr-claims.pdf)). Our own finding that **only about 21% of BI claims are reported at 3 months with a cumulative factor of about 4.85** is internally consistent (1/4.85 = 20.6%) and says the AY2024 BI count is understated by 82%, against 6% for AY2023. Outside BI the count problem is small (motor +7.9%, buildings +5.8%, contents +3.5% for AY2024), so for those lines the larger immaturity issues are on the cost side of the claim, covered in the next section. The same stable link ratios mean a non-parametric count chain ladder is defensible; use a volume-weighted average over the most recent diagonals, drop exceptional cells only with a documented reason, and fit an exponential-decay tail to the BI count links (check that every late link exceeds 1.000), with a Bondy-style repeat of the last link as the stress case (A). Chain ladder at a CDF of 4.85 multiplies any noise in the 21% observed by nearly five, which is exactly the leverage that motivates BF and Cape Cod for immature years ([LossReserves.com](https://lossreserves.com/learn/bornhuetter-ferguson/)).

The implementation decision is how to carry completeness into a Tweedie model, and the evidence favours an offset over scaling the target. Dividing observed cost by completeness cannot add the expected claims to a row with zero reported claims (0 divided by anything is 0), whereas an offset lets the model expect a zero on a low-completeness row and penalises it lightly (our inference from the compound Poisson-gamma form of Tweedie; no source states this non-equivalence explicitly, and the scikit-learn [example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py) supports only the model form). The literature on exposure supports the same distinction: offset and ratio-weighted Tweedie regressions imply weights of t^(2-p) versus t, and they differ materially when t is far below 1 ([Boucher and Coulibaly](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11788)). Treating completeness c as a fractional exposure (my analogy; their paper concerns exposure, not completeness), the ratio form gives immature rows weight c where the likelihood-consistent offset form gives c^0.5 at p = 1.5. If the target is pre-adjusted rather than offset, the weights must be adjusted too ([Shi, CAS Forum 2010](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_10wforum_shi.pdf)). There is a neat consequence: with a Poisson-type likelihood, the offset-only estimate of a constant mean is the sum of reported divided by the sum of exposure times completeness, which is the Cape Cod estimator; so a Tweedie model with a completeness offset is the closest model-based analogue of Cape Cod and BF, with the learned mean playing the role of the a priori (I, standard algebra). It also follows that the offset is not optional decoration: the CAS RPM material warns that applying age-based factors to losses while ignoring that development differs by cell is generally wrong ([RPM 2016](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2016_presentations_mai-3.pdf)), so completeness should vary by line and, for BI, be estimated separately.

Two refinements are specific to this dataset. First, the 2024 immaturity varies inside the year, so completeness belongs at row level: for a row with exposure from s to e, set c as the exposure-weighted average of the line's completeness curve evaluated at (31 Dec 2024 minus day), which makes a Q4 BI row far less complete than a Q1 row (I). Second, count completeness is not dollar completeness. If late-reported BI claims are larger than early ones, the dollar share reported at 3 months is below 21% and the count-based offset under-adjusts; the snapshot can test this because AY2021-22 are essentially mature, so the share of AY incurred coming from claims reported within each lag can be computed directly, valued at the same date, without needing any history (I, flagged by the data-availability notes as an approximation that "is often false" when severity differs by report lag). Where the dollar curve differs, use it instead for BI. Treat the BI tail as the largest single assumption and report a sensitivity range, as the research notes also recommend for the line whose latest-year completeness and tail dominate the forecast.

## Open claims overstate net cost, potentially by more than the missing claims do

The target is incurred minus recoveries excluding declined claims, and the snapshot biases it upward on open claims because recoveries are booked only on closed claims. **Correction (verified on the project data after this report was drafted):** the first draft assumed every open own-damage/theft claim would eventually receive a recovery of about 80% of incurred, giving an overstatement of 3.16 times at a 54% open share. That is wrong on two counts. First, only 28.2% of closed motor own-damage/theft claims carry a recovery (those that do average 77% of incurred), so the expected recovery is 18.5% of incurred on these perils, and an open claim of these perils is overstated by 1/(1-0.185) = 1.23 times, not 5 times. Second, the 54% open share applies to all 2024 Q4 claims, whereas motor own-damage/theft is only 12% of open 2024 incurred (about 7.2M of 60M). The net effect is roughly 7.2M x 0.185 = 1.3M, about 1.5% of the 90.7M as-valued 2024 motor net cost. This is a small, second-order correction, far smaller than the bodily-injury reporting delay (+82% of claim counts) and comparable to the IBNER uncertainty. It can still confound a year effect slightly, so it is worth applying, but it is not the largest bias.

The literature supports the principle if not the exact recipe. Recoveries (salvage and subrogation) must be tracked as separate gross and net data; Friedland's treatment projects them by ratio to paid claims or by development, warns that early-maturity data are thin, and insists on clarity about net versus gross ([Friedland Ch. 14 via BattleActs](https://battleacts.pro.wiki/wiki/Friedland14.Recoveries)); Grace adapted BF to recoveries and noted that insufficient data exist to determine them independently ([CAS Proceedings, Grace](https://casact.org/pubs/proceed/proceed93/93271.pdf)); a 1988 CAS seminar called salvage and subrogation a "potential distorter of loss development patterns" ([CLRS 1988](https://www.casact.org/pubs/CLRSTrans/1988/683.pdf)). None of these sources states that recoveries arrive with claim age or that recording only at closure biases open claims; that step is our inference from the pieces. The recommended handling (A + I) is to build the label claim by claim. For closed claims use incurred minus recorded recoveries. For open own-damage and theft claims use incurred times (1 - rho), where rho is the recovery-to-incurred ratio of closed own-damage and theft claims in the mature accident years, estimated separately for own-damage and theft and checked by report lag, because claims that remain open may recover differently from those that closed quickly. Without a close date in the snapshot we cannot test selection on closure speed directly, which is a risk to state. An equivalent safer alternative is to model gross cost and the recovery rate as separate components; sources agree that claim systems hold recoveries as separate financial objects ([Guidewire docs](https://docs.guidewire.com/cloud/is/202603/cloudapibf/cloudAPI/ClaimCenter/financials/recoveries.html)).

Declined (repudiated) claims behave similarly. They carry zero amounts, and closed-without-payment claims are a heterogeneous class (below-deductible, duplicates, uncovered, withdrawn, denied) that regulators and industry define inconsistently, so counts must be defined identically at training and scoring ([NAIC MCAS handout](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/legacy/mcas_training/2018/MCAS_Property_and_Casualty-Handout.pdf); [Triple-I via MyChesCo](https://www.mychesco.com/?p=670250)). No actuarial text we found handles declined claims in count development, so the rule is ours (I): compute the declined share of reported claims by line in AY2021-22; compare it with AY2023-24 at the snapshot; the shortfall is the share of still-open claims expected to end at zero, and open-claim expectations are multiplied by one minus that probability. In count development, build the completeness curve on all reported claims including declined ones, then apply the decline share separately, so completeness and decline do not contaminate each other.

Deductibles need a short check rather than a method. Net-of-deductible cost is a layer of the ground-up distribution, a fixed deductible erodes under inflation so net cost grows faster than ground-up (leveraged trend), and net amounts probably develop later than ground-up ones ([CAS RPM 2009, Thorpe](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2009_handouts_thorpe.pdf); the development point is inference). We do not know whether incurred is gross or net of the policy excess, or whether claims below it appear as zero-payment closed claims. Confirm from the data dictionary; if net, include the excess as a covariate and note that the training years' trend is leveraged relative to ground-up.

## The 2023 miss may be one claim, not trend

A LightGBM Tweedie model trained on 2021-22 predicted 64-75% of 2023 actual, and the working diagnosis was cost trend. The arithmetic argues for caution. A model effectively predicting the 2021-22 average level (midpoint about 1 Jan 2022) for 2023 (midpoint 1 Jul 2023) spans 1.5 years, so a ratio of 0.64-0.75 needs 21% to 35% annual growth; if it behaves like a 2022-level predictor spanning one year, 33% to 56% (my arithmetic). Recent US motor figures do show repair-cost spikes, with motor-vehicle insurance CPI up 22.6% in the year to April 2024 ([Swiss Re](https://www.swissre.com/reinsurance/insights/global-motor-reinsurance-perspectives/us.html)), but our jurisdiction is unknown and those are single-year price changes. Competing explanations are the **11.9M motor BI claim in 2023 against a typical 10-20k**, exposure growth or mix shift between years, catastrophe days in buildings and contents, and shrinkage in a zero-heavy GBM. The diagnostic is cheap: report actual over predicted per line, split into frequency and severity, with and without that claim and with a capped severity. Only the residual after those removals can be called trend, and checking the in-sample actual/predicted ratio on the training years separates GBM shrinkage from out-of-time shift.

Large losses need an explicit rule because the metric is uncapped. The standard approach caps each claim at a threshold, develops and trends the more stable capped losses, then loads the excess back, dividing by one minus the excess ratio ([Variance: NCCI procedure](https://variancejournal.org/article/84910-catastrophes-and-workers-compensation-ratemaking)); candidate thresholds are the basic limit, a reinsurance retention, a share of the coverage limit or the 99th percentile of claim size ([BattleActs: Werner and Modlin Ch. 6](https://battleacts.pro.wiki/wiki/Werner06.LossLAE)). A CAS seminar design separates frequency, capped severity, probability of an excess claim and excess severity, and another comparison found frequency-severity and loss-cost relativities close once capping and exposure treatments match ([CAS RPM 2011, Yan](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2011_handouts_pm13-yan.pdf)). Capped and ground-up triangles need matched factors, since ground-up factors applied to capped data over-develop and removing large losses before applying uncapped factors understates ultimate ([Adams, CAS Forum 2013](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_13fforum_03-adams.pdf)); this matters less for us because we have counts, not dollar development factors, but the cap must be applied before any cell-level scaling. Large claims also report and settle later, so immature years under-represent large claims not yet over the threshold ([UCLouvain DIAL working paper](https://research.dial.uclouvain.be/bitstreams/8d7bd8a2-df50-4bca-b08e-79a7ab469134/download)). Our recommendation (A): cap per claim by line at about the 99th-99.5th percentile of positive pooled AY2021-24 severities, chosen so the excess share is stable across years; fit the Tweedie model on the capped target; and add back a multiplicative excess load per line equal to pooled excess over pooled capped cost. Omitting the load biases predictions low by roughly the excess ratio, and a Huber-type loss or winsorising without a load estimates something below the conditional mean (I). The 11.9M claim is one observation in four years of BI, so decide explicitly whether the load is the pooled empirical value (which treats it as a once-in-four-years event) or a smoothed fitted tail; first check that it is not a data error (a shifted decimal, a policy-limit case reserve that is still open and may still move).

The cost of ignoring this is large under the chosen score. At power 1.5 the unit deviance is 4y/√ŷ - 8√y + 4√ŷ, homogeneous of degree 0.5 in scale ([scikit-learn metric docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)). If the modelled mean for that claim's row were 1,000, a miss of 11.9M adds about 1.48 million deviance units, equal to roughly 11,700 ordinary zero-claim rows each contributing about 126 (illustrative, my arithmetic). A fold containing it measures mostly whether that claim is predicted, which no feature model can do. Report pooled D2 with and without it and by line.

Catastrophe handling follows the same logic. Practice strips actual catastrophe losses from the experience period and substitutes an expected annual load, because event years bias a short window, and ASOP 39 says the presence or absence of catastrophes biases cost estimates ([ASB, ASOP 39](https://www.actuarialstandardsboard.org/asops/treatment-catastrophe-losses-propertycasualty-insurance-ratemaking/); [Milliman](https://www.milliman.com/en/insight/wildfire-catastrophe-models-california-ratemaking)). A revision has been through exposure drafts, and its final status was not confirmed. With four years and a target that includes whatever happens in 2025, no credible load can be derived from our data alone; practical handling (I): flag days with abnormal buildings/contents claim clusters, check how much of any year's buildings cost sits there, cap per event day where possible, and keep a long-run average in the level rather than letting one event year steer postcode relativities.

Trend must be explicit because trees cannot extrapolate it: a tree model can only return values seen in training leaves, so it repeats the latest training year's level for the forecast year (I; the modelling notes flag no source for this and call it background practice). The standard form is a log-linear fit to accident-year frequency and severity, with an annual factor from the slope, applied from each period's average accident date to that of the forecast period; the midpoint convention is documented ([Medina, CAS E-Forum](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_11wforumpt2_medina.pdf); [CAS Proceedings 70](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/proceed_proceed70_70001.pdf)), and trend reports compare several window lengths before a judgmental selection ([Oliver Wyman trend report](https://nserbt.ca/sites/default/files/OW_Trend_Report_%28Signed%29_-_Comm_-_Dec_2015_Data.pdf)). Do this on developed counts and capped severity, never on calendar-year data (exposure growth distorts it, per Medina) and never on raw AY2024 cost, whose immaturity would bias the slope low unless the completeness offset enters first (I). With four points the slope has two degrees of freedom, so shrink toward a pooled or external estimate and allow line-specific values only where the evidence is clear. The model log-link then takes the trend as an offset (XGBoost's base_margin, LightGBM's init_score), though the learned trees depend on the offset, so the same offset must be reproduced at prediction time ([XGBoost docs](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/intercept.html)).

## Validate at the maturity the labels really have

Out-of-time folds mimic the forecast direction, but the labels in each fold carry a different amount of immaturity. Fold 1 validates on AY2023, nearly complete on counts (+0.6% motor), so its main issues are the large claim and trend. Fold 2 validates on AY2024, so scoring the raw labels would reward a model that under-predicts; two scorings are needed (I, built on the scoring-rule properties in the [scikit-learn docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)). The first is observed-space: compare c times the prediction with the observed label, which needs no imputation but inherits the completeness assumption and so cannot detect a wrong c. The second is developed-space: compare predictions with labels whose recoveries and declines are replaced by expectations, which is exposed to the same errors as the correction itself ([Crevecoeur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07145) criticise treating imputed values as observed). Agreement of both is reassuring; disagreement points at the correction rather than the model. Because the deviance has degree 0.5 in scale, scoring unweighted against labels divided by c gives each row c^-0.5 times its observed-space deviance, so noisy low-c rows are up-weighted; keep the offset form for both fitting and scoring and use developed-space only as a cross-check. A re-cut to equal maturity is possible and valuable: using report_date, truncate each accident period to the claims reported within k months and check that the completeness curve predicts the claims reported later in mature years.

The scoring details need discipline. D2 uses the hold-out year's own mean as its null, so a fold with an outlier has a larger null deviance as well as a larger model deviance. D2 and Gini answer different questions, since Gini ignores level ([scikit-learn example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)); report calibration (actual/predicted by line and decile) beside both. Train with power 1.5 to match the metric; the CAS handout approach of choosing the power by likelihood over a grid applies to the model, not the scoring ([Yan, CAS Spring 2017](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/spring_2017_presentations_c-12_yan.pdf)); test 1.3-1.7 as sensitivity on out-of-time folds. Rebalancing is cheap insurance: after fitting, compute actual over predicted by line in the out-of-time fold on the corrected basis and apply a shrunk scalar to the final forecast. A scalar leaves ranking, hence Gini, unchanged but moves deviance (I). With only two single-year folds the scalar is noisy, and a Cape Cod style benchmark gives it a sanity check: per line and accident year, compute pure premium as the sum of corrected net cost over the sum of exposure times completeness, trend it to 2025, and make the model's aggregate 2025 prediction reconcile to this within a stated tolerance.

## Conclusion

The practical lesson is that "immature data" is three different problems wearing one name. Unreported claims are a measurable count problem, solved by a row-level completeness offset. Unbooked recoveries and undecided declines are a small label-definition problem (about 1.5% of 2024 motor cost for recoveries, per our data check), much smaller than the unreported-claims problem in bodily injury. Case-estimate development is an unmeasurable problem on one snapshot and must be carried as an assumption with a range. Fixing them in the wrong order, or letting a year feature absorb them, would produce a trend estimate that is really an artefact of maturity and open-claim conventions.

What would change this plan is the answer to the first priority item. If the 2025 labels are scored at an early maturity with the same open-claim conventions as our snapshot, the right target is the observed-at-that-maturity net cost, and the recovery and decline corrections would move from the label into the model's expected value only through the maturity itself. We also remain honest about the evidence: the structure of the method is well supported by textbook practice as summarised in secondary sources, but the specific recipes for single-snapshot data, recovery recording bias, decline development and tree-model trend handling are our inferences, and each should be verified in Friedland, Werner and Modlin and CAS Monograph 5 before being presented as sourced.

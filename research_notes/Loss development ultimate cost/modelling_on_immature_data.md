## 1. Methods to put claims on an ultimate basis before modelling (develop to ultimate, completeness offsets, exclusion of immature periods, explicit reporting-delay / micro-level models)

### Takeaway
The literature describes the standard pricing practice as a two-step process: estimate ultimate claim counts and sizes for the incomplete data, then model on those estimates as if they were observed. The Crevecoeur/Antonio et al. paper (ASTIN Bulletin 2023) criticises this because it ignores the uncertainty in the imputed values, and proposes a joint occurrence-and-development model. Individual-claim reserving work (Antonio & Plat; Verrall, Nielsen & Jessen; Verrall & Wüthrich; Wüthrich) provides the reporting-delay machinery (occurrence process x reporting-delay distribution) from which an expected-reported-share ("completeness") factor can be derived per accident period. Most ML individual-reserving papers focus on RBNS (reported) claims and do not directly model IBNR (not yet reported) claims at claim level.

### Cited Findings
- Pricing actuaries only observe reported claims, not all claims that occurred in past exposure periods because of reporting delay. Amounts paid on open claims also understate ultimate loss. The standard fix is two-step: estimate counts and severities at the evaluation date, then feed these into the pricing model as if observed, which neglects their uncertainty. — [Crevecoeur, Antonio, Desmedt, Masquelein, "Bridging the gap between pricing and reserving with an occurrence and development model for non-life insurance claims", ASTIN Bulletin 53(2), 2023 (search summary of abstract; arXiv 2203.07145)](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07145); also [UvA DARE record](https://dare.uva.nl/id/52185241-9f55-4f57-9891-124795095445)
- The same paper builds a granular occurrence and development model to remove the inconsistency between observed data and imputed estimates in pricing; applied to an insurance and a reinsurance portfolio; benefits were most pronounced in the reinsurance case with long reporting and settlement lags, low frequency and heavy-tailed claim sizes (search summary). — [CAS-hosted copy of the paper](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2024-09/Bridging_the_gap_between_pricing_and_reserving_with_an_occurrence_and_development_model_for_non-life.pdf)
- Micro-level reserving (Antonio & Plat 2014, Scandinavian Actuarial Journal) models each event in a claim's life (occurrence, reporting delay, payments, settlement) with position-dependent marked Poisson processes. RBNS claims are projected from estimated hazard rates; IBNR claims are estimated by simulating how many unreported claims appear, using a filtered Poisson process combined with the reporting-delay distribution. In the case study (European liability claims) the micro-level model outperformed aggregate-data methods (search summary; full text paywalled). — [KU Leuven repository / search result](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/186233); [Antonio CAS seminar handout 2013](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/clrs_2013_handouts_paper_2370_handout_1331_0.pdf)
- Verrall, Nielsen & Jessen (2010) model reserves at individual-claim level, approximated with a compound Poisson framework; they estimate the reporting delay (IBNR delay) and payment delay (RBNS delay) separately and compare with chain ladder (search summary). — [Search result: prediction of RBNS and IBNR claims using claim amounts and claim counts](https://casact.org/abstract/prediction-rbns-and-ibnr-claims-using-claim-amounts-and-claim-counts)
- Verrall & Wüthrich (2016) were reported as the first to present an IBNR count model at daily level, including heterogeneity in reporting delay by occurrence date and a strong weekday pattern; Verbelen et al. extended the weekday pattern to delays beyond one week and presented EM as a framework for jointly estimating the occurrence and reporting processes (search summary via a 2018 arXiv paper). — [Modeling the number of hidden events subject to observation delay, arXiv 1801.02935](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1801.02935)
- Wüthrich (2018, Scandinavian Actuarial Journal 2018(6), 465-480, "Machine learning in individual claims reserving") is cited as the first application of a tree-based method (CART) to individual loss reserving. It predicts the number of payments for RBNS claims using claim features; IBNR claims have no claim-specific information, so no individual prediction is possible for them (search summary via Variance journal). A Scribd-hosted secondary source says Wüthrich assumed a homogeneous marked Poisson process for occurrence/reporting and predicted IBNR counts with chain ladder; this is unverified. — [Variance: comparison of two individual tree-based loss reserving methods](https://variancejournal.org/article/130025); [Scribd secondary](https://www.scribd.com/document/378808587/Reserving)
- Follow-ups: Duval & Pigeon (2019) use gradient boosting for individual reserving; De Felice & Moriconi (2019) use a CART-based claim-watching model extending Wüthrich to cost/ultimate prediction (search summary). — [Risks 2020, 8(2), 33 (IDEAS)](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v8y2020i2p33-d342279.html); [Risks 2019, 7(4), 102](https://0-www-mdpi-com.brum.beds.ac.uk/2227-9091/7/4/102)
- Neural-network individual reserving: Delong, Lindholm & Wüthrich (JORI 2021) use six neural networks on a monthly grid to predict settlement processes and benchmark against chain ladder; Delong & Wüthrich use one network to jointly model all payment-delay periods for RBNS reserves; Chaoubi et al. (2022) use an LSTM in a discrete-time individual framework. A Wüthrich preprint (Feb 2026) proposes multi-period factors projecting latest observations directly to ultimate (search summary only; not verified). — [Delong et al. / search result](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s13385-021-00271-4); [Chaoubi et al., arXiv 2201.13267](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2201.13267); [arXiv 2602.15385](https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.15385v2)
- Multinomial micro-level model (mCube): each accident year has a probability that a claim is reported a given number of years later; IBNR counts = claims not yet reported in the observed triangle; the specification is consistent with chain ladder (search summary). — [mCube, arXiv 2212.00101](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00101)
- Schnieper-type models treat newly reported claims as arriving in proportion to exposure and treat changes in cost of already-reported claims as a separate process; aimed at pricing high-deductible layers with scarce data (search summary). — [Schnieper, ASTIN Bulletin vol 21 no 1](https://www.casact.org/library/astin/vol21no1/111.pdf); [arXiv 2603.11258](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.11258)
- A CAS E-Forum paper notes the ultimate cost of an unpaid individual claim follows a probability distribution rather than a point from an LDF, and applying loss development factors to individual claims often creates biased estimates of excess loss costs (search summary; could not identify author/title). — [CAS Forum Spring 2017 vol 2](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_17spforumv2_completespring2017vol2.pdf)
- A CAS RPM 2016 presentation argues reserve development matters for pricing, and that common shortcuts such as applying age-based factors to losses and reducing exposure by age generally assume all development is the same, which is often wrong (search summary). — [RPM 2016 presentation](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2016_presentations_mai-3.pdf)
- A GLM can take external ultimate-loss estimates as offset terms and, with a particular specification, reproduces Mack's chain-ladder development pattern (Bornhuetter-Ferguson as GLM; search summary). — [Search result list, e.g. IDEAS/arXiv-linked BF-GLM paper](https://arts.units.it/handle/11368/2298159)
- Case reserves are an input to pricing analysis, in aggregate for indications/relativities and in detail for predictive modelling (NAIC handout, search summary). — [NAIC book club handout](https://content.naic.org/sites/default/files/call_materials/Presentation%20Handout%20-%20Actuarial%20Case%20Reserves%20-%20NAIC%20Book%20Club.pdf)

### Inferences
- Menu of defensible options, from least to most model-heavy: (a) fit only on mature accident years; (b) develop the target to ultimate by cell-level LDFs (line x accident-year x peril/report-lag) applied to paid/incurred; (c) keep the observed target and put the expected reported/developed share into the model as an offset/weight; (d) claim-level reporting-delay model that yields a per-policy-period probability that a claim has been reported by the valuation date. (c) and (d) are the same idea at different granularity.
- Because the hackathon snapshot has a report_date per claim, the reporting-delay distribution by line/peril can be estimated directly from the reported claims (delay = report_date - accident date), with right-truncation at the valuation date handled in the likelihood or by chain-ladder-style count triangles. This gives the completeness factor c(accident period, line, peril) without needing external patterns.
- For the slow-reporting bodily-injury peril, a completeness factor from a few mature years carries a lot of extrapolation (tail) risk; the literature on reinsurance-like cases (long lags, heavy tails) shows the largest benefit from explicit modelling, so this line is where choice of method matters most.
- Using development factors on individual claims is flagged as biased for large claims; prefer applying factors at cell level (or to capped claims) rather than per claim.

No source found that describes a published "completeness offset in a Tweedie GBM" recipe specifically.

## 2. Frequency-severity vs direct Tweedie on immature data; unreported (zero) rows; why scaling observed cost is not equivalent to a completeness offset

### Takeaway
Frequency-severity separates the two incompleteness mechanisms (unreported claims affect frequency; open claims still developing affect severity), so each can get its own correction; a direct Tweedie pure-premium model mixes both into one target and needs a single combined completeness factor. For rows with no reported claim, scaling an observed amount (0 x factor = 0) cannot add the missing expected claims, whereas an offset/weight changes the expected value the model is allowed to predict for that row; no source I found states this explicitly, so it rests on the Tweedie/Poisson properties below.

### Cited Findings
- scikit-learn's insurance example presents the two approaches: (1) Poisson frequency x Gamma severity, multiplied; (2) Tweedie on total claim amount per exposure with power in (1,2), which is the compound Poisson-gamma. It notes the directly fitted compound Poisson-gamma model is operationally simpler (one estimator, one set of hyper-parameters), and that Gini measures ranking only, not calibration. — [scikit-learn Tweedie insurance example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)
- In that example frequency = ClaimNb/Exposure is fitted with Poisson and Exposure as sample_weight, and severity is fitted on rows with claims only, weighted by claim count; scikit-learn's GLM docs state the same relative-frequency construction (counts/exposure as target, exposure as weight). — [example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py); [scikit-learn linear_model docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/linear_model.rst)
- Boucher & Coulibaly (arXiv 2502.11788) compare the "offset" and "ratio (target = loss/exposure, weight = exposure)" formulations of Tweedie regression for partial-year exposure. The offset approach is asymptotically more efficient; the ratio approach does better on portfolio-level financial balance when exposures are heterogeneous/truncated. The implied weights are t^(2-p) (offset) vs t (ratio) for p in (1,2), t in (0,1]; they coincide at t=1 and converge as p approaches 1 (Poisson) (search summary, abstract-level). — [arXiv 2502.11788](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.11788)
- Shi (CAS E-Forum 2010): when the response is pre-adjusted by known factors in a direct Tweedie analysis with log link, the exposure weights should also be adjusted (search summary). — [Shi, CAS Forum Winter 2010](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_10wforum_shi.pdf); [abstract](https://casact.org/abstract/direct-analysis-pre-adjusted-loss-cost-frequency-or-severity-tweedie-models)
- Practitioner source: scikit-learn's TweedieRegressor has no offset argument, so putting log exposure in as a feature gives a coefficient that is not fixed at 1 (practitioner blog, search summary). — [burning-cost.github.io post](https://burning-cost.github.io/2026/03/21/tweedie-regression-insurance-what-sklearn-doesnt-tell-you/)
- Offsets in boosting libraries: XGBoost's base_margin is the analogue of a GLM offset; for LightGBM set_init_score is the equivalent (practitioner write-up). The same discussion warns that with a base margin the learned trees depend on the offset, so output is not simply f(features)+offset in a fixed-function sense (search summary). — [XGBoost intercept tutorial](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/intercept.html); [Medium: offsetting, logic to implementation](https://medium.com/data-science/offsetting-the-model-logic-to-implementation-7e333bc25798)
- Compound Poisson-gamma/Tweedie arises from Poisson counts and gamma severities and has a point mass at zero (practitioner/summary sources). — [CAS RPM 2022 "Alternatives to the Tweedie"](https://www.casact.org/sites/default/files/2022-07/RM9_AtlernativestoTweedieDistributioninGLM.pdf); [MetricGate](https://metricgate.com/blogs/tweedie-glm-insurance-pure-premium/)

### Inferences
- Why scaling is not an offset (reasoning): let c in (0,1] be the expected reported share for a row. Observed loss Y_obs has E[Y_obs] = c*mu. (i) For a row with zero reported claims, Y_obs/c = 0 regardless of c, so a "scaled" target says nothing about the unreported claims that are in expectation (1-c)*mu; the information that this zero is less informative when c is low is lost. (ii) Under the offset formulation the model fits E[Y_obs] = exp(log c + eta) with the Tweedie variance function evaluated at the observed mean, so a low-c zero is a likely outcome and costs little deviance. (iii) Scaling up only the reported amounts inflates the positive tail relative to zeros, over-weights a few developed large claims, and creates a target whose zero-mass and variance no longer match a compound Poisson-gamma. Scaling the target by 1/c and passing c as weight is the "ratio" form; per Boucher & Coulibaly's analogy (exposure t ~ completeness c) it implies weight c vs c^(2-p) for the offset form, i.e. it under-weights immature rows relative to the likelihood-consistent form (by-analogy inference; their paper concerns exposure, not completeness).
- Frequency-severity advantage on immature data: counts can be completed with a reporting-delay-based c_count (Poisson with offset log c_count), and severity can be developed using claim-level or cell-level case-incurred/paid factors on claims only; zero rows never need a severity adjustment. A direct Tweedie needs a blended c that varies with both mechanisms and with line/peril; where reporting is slow (BI), c_count and c_severity differ materially and a single factor is crude.
- Pragmatic hybrid for a Tweedie-only requirement: build the target as developed-to-ultimate (severity-side development applied at claim/cell level) and include c_count as an offset/weight so that zero rows with low c are down-weighted/expected to be non-zero.

### Gaps
- No source found that explicitly shows the scaled-target-vs-offset non-equivalence for rows with zero reported claims, or that tests a completeness offset in a Tweedie GBM. This is flagged as inference only.
- Did not locate a CAS paper comparing frequency-severity vs Tweedie specifically under incomplete data (the Cambridge "Frequency/severity versus pure premium modeling" chapter appeared in search results but was not read).

## 3. Validation design for forecasting a future period (out-of-time folds; observed-space vs developed-actual validation; bias risks)

### Takeaway
No source in the retrieved material prescribes this for immature data. Reasoning from the scikit-learn Tweedie properties: deviance is a homogeneous function of degree 2-p, so scaling predictions by c versus scaling actuals by 1/c is not neutral for p=1.5; and observed-space validation inherits the same completeness assumption as the fit, so it cannot detect a wrong c. Use both, and keep a time-based hold-out.

### Cited Findings
- scikit-learn: mean Tweedie deviance is a homogeneous function of degree 2-power; for power=2 (Gamma) simultaneous scaling of y_true and y_pred has no effect, for Poisson (power=1) the deviance scales linearly, for Normal quadratically. — [scikit-learn model_evaluation docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- The metric "elicits predicted expectation values of regression targets" (i.e. it is a consistent scoring rule for the mean), so a mis-scaled level is penalised. — [model_evaluation.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- scikit-learn: when cross-validating the `power` of TweedieRegressor specify an explicit scoring function because the default scorer depends on `power` itself; the example compares models over a grid of power values for the same reason. — [linear_model.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/linear_model.rst); [example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)
- Gini/Lorenz curves rank only and are blind to calibration: any monotonic transformation leaves the Gini unchanged; even an oracle cannot reach 1.0. — [example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)
- The CAS Monograph 5 (Goldburd, Khare, Tevet, [Guller in 2nd ed. 2025]) has chapters on data preparation and model validation; the search did not surface its text on out-of-time validation or capping. — [CAS Monograph No. 5 page](https://www.casact.org/monograph/cas-monograph-no-5)
- Crevecoeur et al. note that treating imputed estimates as observed outcomes ignores their uncertainty (applies to validating against developed actuals too). — [arXiv 2203.07145](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07145)

### Inferences
- Observed-space validation: compare c_i * pred_i (the predicted reported loss) with the observed immature y_i using Tweedie deviance, ideally with the same offset/weights used in fitting. Advantages: no imputation of the unobserved; consistent with an offset model. Bias risk: relies on the same c; if c is overstated for recent months, a model that over-predicts level looks fine in observed space but is too high on an ultimate basis; also immature rows contribute little information, so do the evaluation on the most mature slice where possible.
- Developed-actual validation: compare pred_i to y_i / c_i (or to cell-level ultimate). Bias risk: you evaluate against your own estimate; for p=1.5 deviance scales as c^(2-p) = c^0.5, so the ratio form up-weights noisy low-c rows relative to the offset form; large claims developed with a cell LDF create outliers; zero rows remain zero (see Section 2).
- Design suggestion for a single-snapshot problem: (1) hold out the most recent fully-observed accident year(s) as the out-of-time fold, with earlier years training, mirroring the forecast direction; (2) because the snapshot has uneven maturity across years, also report an "equal-maturity" evaluation by truncating all years to the same age using report_date (re-cut reporting as of accident-date + k months) so the target is comparable across years; (3) score both calibration (actual/predicted by decile and by line) and rank (Gini, deviance); (4) compute D2 or deviance per line, not only pooled.
- The "truncate to equal maturity using report_date" idea is only possible because report_date is in the data; it gives a retrospective triangle and lets one validate completeness factors themselves (predict later-reported claims).

### Gaps
- No retrieved publication that compares observed-space and developed-actual validation for pricing models on immature data. The above is design reasoning, not literature.
- CAS Monograph 5 text on out-of-time validation not accessible.

## 4. Trend handling: explicit trend factors/offsets, year as feature vs offset, tree extrapolation, calibration/rebalancing

### Takeaway
I found no retrieved source that explicitly documents these points; the standard-practice statements in this section are from my background knowledge and are not cited (see Gaps). What the sources do support: boosting offsets (XGBoost base_margin / LightGBM init_score) are the mechanism for injecting a fixed trend or completeness term; the base margin changes what the trees learn; and Gini is blind to level so a separate calibration check is needed.

### Cited Findings
- XGBoost base_margin is the analogue of a GLM offset; learning starts from the margin, so tree outputs depend on the offsets. — [XGBoost intercept tutorial](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/intercept.html); [XGBoost discussion thread](https://discuss.xgboost.ai/t/how-does-offset-in-xgboost-is-handled-in-binary-logistic-objective-function/775)
- LightGBM equivalent is set_init_score (practitioner write-up; the 'init_score only in fit' remark is from an older version and may be outdated). — [Medium: offsetting](https://medium.com/data-science/offsetting-the-model-logic-to-implementation-7e333bc25798); [Numerai forum](https://forum.numer.ai/t/custom-objective-for-lightgbm/4677/12)
- Gini does not reflect calibration: any monotone transformation of predictions leaves it unchanged. — [scikit-learn example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)
- Shi (2010): if the response is pre-adjusted by known factors under a log-link Tweedie model, the weights must be adjusted too. This is the relevant rule if trend is applied to the target (pre-adjustment) rather than as an offset. — [Shi, CAS Forum 2010](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/database/forum_10wforum_shi.pdf)
- A search on tree-model extrapolation of year effects returned a Swedish study where GBM was consistently selected as best by out-of-sample measures, and a paper on approximating GBMs by GLMs for tariff use, but nothing on year-trend extrapolation (search summary). — [Swedish Actuarial Society ML presentation](https://aktuarieforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/20181122-SAf-ML-presentation-FINAL.pdf); [Henckaerts et al., arXiv 1904.10890](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.10890)

### Inferences
- Practical design for a log-link model: put the trend as log(trend_factor[AY]) in the offset (GLM) or base_margin/init_score (GBM) with factors estimated from a simple GLM (year as categorical or linear, excluding the immature target problem by using developed/complete data) or from external indices. Do not give a GBM the raw year as a feature to project forward: a tree can only output values seen in training leaves, so the newest training year's level is repeated for the forecast year; any trend from the last training year to the forecast year must be added by an explicit factor.
- Validation of trend: compare level of predictions vs actuals on the held-out year per line, and compute a global (or per-line) scalar = sum(actual)/sum(predicted) on the out-of-time year; apply it as a rebalancing multiplier to the final forecast (a scalar does not change ranking so Gini is unaffected but deviance and bias are). With only four accident years, trend estimates are noisy: prefer shrunk or shared trend across lines unless a line shows a clear difference, and treat slow-reporting peril differently as its latest year is least complete.
- Danger of confounding: for immature data, apparent year effects mix real trend with unmodelled incompleteness (recent years look cheaper). If year is a feature or a free trend, it will absorb incompleteness; the completeness offset must be in the model first, or the trend estimate will be biased low.

### Gaps
- No cited source on (a) tree models failing to extrapolate time effects; (b) year-as-feature vs offset practice; (c) calibration/rebalancing on out-of-time data; (d) typical trend selection methods. These are standard-practice statements from my own background and should be verified in the CAS Monograph 5 / pricing literature before being presented as sourced.

## 5. Tweedie deviance (p=1.5) and D2 with heavy tails; calibration and extreme-loss sensitivity; capping/winsorising; ensembling

### Takeaway
Tweedie deviance with 1<p<2 down-weights large deviations relative to MSE (higher power gives less weight to extreme deviations) but is still level-sensitive and, being scale-dependent at p=1.5, is dominated by large claims in absolute terms. D2 is the fraction of deviance explained versus the intercept-only model and can be negative, so a model that is mis-calibrated in level or hit by an extreme loss can score below the null. Capping/excess-loss redistribution is common in practice; the retrieved sources only document the mechanics in one R package.

### Cited Findings
- Mean Tweedie deviance formula for 1<p<2 (the "otherwise" case): 2*( max(y,0)^(2-p)/((1-p)(2-p)) - y*yhat^(1-p)/(1-p) + yhat^(2-p)/(2-p) ). power=0 is MSE, 1 Poisson, 2 Gamma. — [scikit-learn model_evaluation docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- "In general, the higher power the less weight is given to extreme deviations between true and predicted targets." The docs illustrate with predictions 50% above truth for y=1 and y=100: power=0 gives 0.25 vs 2500; power=1 gives 0.189 vs 18.9; power=2 gives 0.144 for both (relative errors only). Interpolating, p=1.5 is between the Poisson and Gamma behaviour; a 50% error on a claim 100x larger counts for 100^(2-1.5)=10x as much. (The 10x is my arithmetic from the degree-(2-p) homogeneity, which the docs state.) — [model_evaluation.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- D2 = 1 - dev(y, yhat)/dev(y, y_null), with y_null the intercept-only optimal prediction (mean of y_true for Tweedie). Best 1.0, can be negative, a constant model predicting y_null scores 0.0; D2 Tweedie is related to McFadden's likelihood ratio index; for power=0 it equals R2. — [model_evaluation.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- d2_tweedie_score(y_true, y_pred, *, sample_weight=None, power=0); power must be <=0 or >=1; added in scikit-learn 1.0 (search summary of docs). — [d2_tweedie_score docs v1.5](https://scikit-learn.org/1.5/modules/generated/sklearn.metrics.d2_tweedie_score.html)
- scikit-learn docs note y_null in D2 is computed from the y_true passed in (the evaluation sample), so D2 on a hold-out year uses that year's mean as null, not the training mean (stated as "mean of y_true"). — [model_evaluation.rst](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)
- The sklearn example fits a Tweedie power of 1.9 in its pure-premium model and compares models over a power grid since the true power is unknown. A CAS handout suggests treating p as estimated or testing a sequence of p values and choosing the max log-likelihood (search summary). — [example source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py); [CAS handout (RPM 2012)](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2012_handouts_session_4738_presentation_1068_0.pdf)
- Capping mechanics: the R package insurancerating offers redistribute_excess_loss(): after choosing a threshold, large losses are capped and the excess is either redistributed to a claim value for the severity model or kept as a separate excess loading per exposure unit; total excess loss is preserved (search summary). — [insurancerating pricing workflow](https://rdrr.io/cran/insurancerating/f/inst/doc/pricing-workflow-building-blocks.Rmd)
- Boosted Tweedie for very zero-heavy data: Tweedie gradient boosting for extremely unbalanced zero-inflated data (Yang, Qian & Zou); excerpt did not discuss IBNR or reporting lag. Another paper discusses boosting under Poisson deviance with log link by adapting weights at each step (search summaries). — [arXiv 1811.10192](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.10192); [arXiv 1904.10890](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1904.10890)
- Probabilistic gradient boosting for frequency and severity (distributional) exists as a recent alternative (search result only). — [arXiv 2412.14916](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.14916)
- Henckaerts et al. and a Swedish practitioner study report GBM as the best performing approach on out-of-sample measures, which supports GBM (with a GLM benchmark) in the ensemble (search summary). — [Swedish Actuarial Society](https://aktuarieforeningen.se/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/20181122-SAf-ML-presentation-FINAL.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because the deviance is scale-sensitive at p=1.5, a handful of very large (or very late-developed) claims will dominate hold-out deviance and D2; report D2 both with and without capped targets, and report Gini and A/E by line alongside it.
- Capping the target at a per-claim threshold (per line) and adding a separate excess load (by line, from the capped-away mass) keeps the Tweedie fit stable and preserves total expected loss; this matters most for BI, where large claims also develop slowest. Winsorising without an excess load biases level downward, which the calibration scalar must then repair.
- Ensembling (e.g. average of a GLM and a GBM on the log scale, or on the mean scale) gives variance reduction; with a Tweedie objective the blend should be checked for calibration afterwards and re-scaled with a single scalar on out-of-time data.
- Hyper-parameter choice of Tweedie power: choose p by out-of-time deviance across a grid (1.2-1.8) rather than by in-sample likelihood; with immature data the zero mass is inflated, which may push the apparent best p toward 1 (more mass at zero) unless the completeness offset is included. (Reasoned, not sourced.)

## 6. Practical recommendations for the hackathon-style problem (single valuation snapshot, claim-level data with report_date and paid/incurred, four accident years, three lines, one very slow-reporting BI peril)

### Takeaway
Everything in this section is synthesis of the cited material above, not sourced advice. A defensible recipe: derive reporting-delay completeness factors per line/peril from report_date, put them in the Tweedie model as an offset (GLM and GBM base_margin/init_score) rather than scaling the target, develop claim severities on a cell basis for open claims, cap large claims with an excess load, validate on an out-of-time accident year plus an equal-maturity re-cut, and finish with a per-line calibration scalar.

### Cited Findings
- All underlying facts are cited in Sections 1-5; key supports: two-step vs joint estimation critique ([Crevecoeur et al.](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2203.07145)); reporting delay x occurrence decomposition for IBNR ([Antonio & Plat summary](https://lirias.kuleuven.be/retrieve/186233), [mCube](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.00101)); offsets in XGBoost/LightGBM ([XGBoost docs](https://xgboost.readthedocs.io/en/stable/tutorials/intercept.html)); Tweedie deviance and D2 properties ([scikit-learn docs source](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/doc/modules/model_evaluation.rst)); GLM vs direct Tweedie comparison ([scikit-learn example](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/scikit-learn/scikit-learn/main/examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.py)).

### Inferences
Suggested recipe, in order:
1. Estimate reporting-delay distribution per line (and BI separately) from report_date minus accident date; build a count triangle by accident month x report lag; get c_count(accident period, line) = expected share already reported at the snapshot. For BI, check whether the tail is supported by the oldest accident year; if not, use a conservative parametric tail (e.g. fitted delay distribution) and sensitivity-test it.
2. For severity development, use cell-level (line x peril x accident year) paid-to-ultimate or incurred-to-ultimate factors on open claims; avoid per-claim LDFs for large claims.
3. Fit Tweedie (p chosen on out-of-time grid, ~1.5 as default) with log(c) as offset (GLM) or base_margin/init_score (GBM) so unreported-zero rows are treated as expected zeros; keep a trend offset by accident year outside the trees.
4. Compare against simpler fallbacks (fit on mature years only; ratio form with weight c) as sensitivity checks.
5. Validate: out-of-time (last complete accident year held out), equal-maturity re-cut, A/E by line/decile, D2 and Gini; report capped and uncapped.
6. Final: ensemble GLM+GBM, one scalar per line to rebalance on the out-of-time year, and an excess load if capped.
- Highest risk is the BI peril: slow reporting means the latest year's completeness factor and the tail dominate the forecast; make it a clearly stated assumption with a sensitivity range.

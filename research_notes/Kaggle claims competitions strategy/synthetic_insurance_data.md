## 2. How typical generators build frequency, severity, reporting delay, settlement, recoveries and ultimate; what is noise, decoy or leak

### Takeaway
In SynthETIC (the one generator read in full) every component is a simple parametric draw: Poisson counts from exposure times a per-period rate, a single truncated power-normal claim size, Weibull reporting and settlement delays whose means are deterministic log functions of claim size, and superimposed inflation keyed to occurrence and payment time. Dependence on covariates is by design either absent or an explicit, removable function. This suggests hidden structure in such generators is a short list of identifiable mechanisms, and everything else is independent noise.

### Cited Findings
- Eight modules in sequence: occurrence, claim size, notification delay, settlement delay, payment count, payment sizes, payment times, inflation — [SynthETIC vignette](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/agi-lab/SynthETIC/master/SynthETIC/vignettes/SynthETIC-demo.Rmd) (read in full).
- Frequency: Poisson with mean = exposure in the period x expected frequency per unit exposure; exposure and frequency may vary across periods but not within a period. Occurrence times are uniform within the period (so a non-homogeneous Poisson process only across periods). Alternatives such as negative binomial or zero-truncated Poisson can be plugged in. Demo uses exposure 12,000 and frequency 0.03 per quarter-unit — same vignette (read in full).
- Claim size (default): S^0.2 ~ Normal(9.5, sd 3), left-truncated at 30, with reference claim 200,000. Alternatives offered: Weibull, inverse Gaussian, a gamma GLM with log link on covariates (the vignette example uses age and age squared, mean exp(27 - 0.768 age + 0.008 age^2), gamma shape 10), or bootstrap from real data — same vignette. So covariate dependence of severity is an opt-in module, not the default.
- Reporting (notification) delay: Weibull with CV 0.70; mean (in quarters) = min(3, max(1, 2 - log(size / (0.5 ref)) / 3)), i.e. larger claims are reported faster, bounded between 1 and 3 quarters — same vignette. (Note this is the opposite sign to the "bodily injury is slow" pattern in our data, so SynthETIC's default is not a template for our reporting process.)
- Settlement delay: Weibull with CV 0.60; mean in quarters = a x min(25, max(1, 6 + 4 log(size / (0.1 ref)))); a regime-change term reduces the mean for small claims with occurrence period >= 21 (a "legislative change") — same vignette. Dependence on notification delay is possible but not default.
- Payments: number of partial payments is 1-2 for small claims, 2-3 for medium, at least 4 (geometric, mean capped at 8) for large; last two payments hold a log-increasing share (75% at 0.1 ref), with beta draws of CV 0.03-0.20; inter-payment delays are Weibull and are rescaled to sum exactly to the settlement delay — same vignette.
- Inflation: base inflation 2% p.a. in the demo, plus superimposed inflation (SI) in two forms: by occurrence time (a legislative step that cuts claim size by up to 40% for the smallest claims, for claims under 0.25 ref, after period 20 quarters) and by payment time (30% p.a. for the smallest claims, tapering linearly to zero at claims of 1 ref and above). Inflation is therefore size-dependent, not uniform — same vignette. The authors state the chain-ladder performs very poorly on the resulting data because the data breach chain-ladder assumptions — same vignette.
- Time truncation: payments projected beyond the max development period are forced to the end of it unless adjust = FALSE, which creates an artificial concentration of transactions in the last period — same vignette (a generator-induced artefact worth looking for in any capped data).
- Recoveries, rating-factor GLM structure, hidden risk scores, interactions, customer-level effects and decoy columns do not exist in SynthETIC; the vignette contains no recoveries and no policy-level covariates beyond the optional gamma-GLM example — same vignette (negative finding, read in full).
- Gabrielli-Wuthrich's machine is the contrasting approach: neural nets calibrated to real data, so the dependence structure comes from real data, not from hand-set formulae — [IDEAS entry](https://ideas.repec.org/a/gam/jrisks/v6y2018i2p29-d138840.html) (search snippet).

### Inferences
- A policy-level synthetic dataset like ours (three lines, rating factors, customers, addresses, weather) is likely built from SynthETIC-style building blocks wrapped in a policy simulator: per-policy expected frequency = base rate x product of factor relativities (x possibly a hidden risk score and weather index), claim count ~ Poisson(exposure x lambda), peril drawn by a multinomial, severity per peril from lognormal/gamma/Pareto-type distributions with a year-on-year inflation multiplier, report delay and status from independent per-peril delay distributions. This is an inference from analogy; no source for our generator exists.
- Features in our description fit that pattern. Severity trend +5-8% a year with no frequency trend looks like an explicit inflation index applied to severity only (as in SynthETIC's payment-time inflation). Recoveries on ~28% of closed own-damage/theft claims at ~80% of incurred look like a Bernoulli flag times a fixed share (a rule, not a smooth risk effect). Bodily injury having slow reporting and a huge outlier (11.9M) fits a heavy-tailed peril-specific mixture, with the outlier being a draw from a Pareto-type tail (indistinguishable from error without seeing the generator).
- Columns that are noise-like (surname, email domain, vehicle colour, branch, campaign) are, by usual practice in such simulators, decoys: generated independently of loss. The test is simple: a column is a decoy if it has no effect after controlling for the true factors. This is common design practice, not something the cited sources say.
- A column generated by claims in the training data but only at baseline in test (customer_service_contacts) is a classic leakage column: in training it is a consequence of the outcome, so it carries target information that does not exist at test time. Generator authors would plausibly add such columns deliberately as traps; this is an inference, and the observation itself comes from our own data, not from a cited source.
- Rows "declined by the insurer" carrying 24-52% of cost is a status/selection mechanism that is probably generated after the claim, so it should not be treated as a rating factor.

### Gaps
- No source describing how recoveries, claim status (closed/open/repudiated) or "ultimate" targets are simulated in any public generator.
- The Gabrielli-Wuthrich paper and the SynthETIC paper text were not read, so detail on case estimates and SPLICE incurred-loss logic is absent.
- No source on typical decoy-column design in actuarial hackathons; the decoy/leak discussion is inference.

## 3. Implications for modelling: GLM versus GBM, interactions, signal ceiling on D2, use of weather/address/customer tables

### Takeaway
No source quantifies the signal ceiling for this data. The general design logic of such generators (few multiplicative factors, independent Poisson noise, heavy-tailed independent severities) implies that a multiplicative GLM with the right factors captures most learnable signal, a shallow GBM adds only where hidden interactions or non-linearities were coded in, and the D2 ceiling is low because Poisson/heavy-tail noise dominates. All of this is inference.

### Cited Findings
- The competition metric is D2 Tweedie: "fraction of Tweedie deviance explained", as defined in scikit-learn — [Kaggle API tag description](https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/competitions/list?search=IAA) (read in full).
- scikit-learn's published example on freMTPL2 (real data) fits Poisson, Gamma and Tweedie models by joining frequency and severity tables — [scikit-learn example](https://scikit-learn.org/stable/auto_examples/linear_model/plot_tweedie_regression_insurance_claims.html) (search snippet). That example is for real data and the snippet did not give the D2 values, so no ceiling can be read from it.
- A hybrid tree-model paper generated synthetic Tweedie-like data with added noise so tree/GLM comparisons would be fair — [arXiv 2006.05617](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2006.05617) (search snippet).
- A Kaggle competition winner for the workers' compensation data reportedly used tree-based models and feature engineering including text features from claim descriptions (injury position by string search) — [Actuaries Institute write-up](https://www.actuaries.digital/2021/10/06/kaggle-competition-modelling-of-claims-costs-and-a-deep-dive-into-the-winning-solution/) (search snippet; article not read). Here the signal sat in a free-text column, which is a different situation from ours.
- SynthETIC's own exposure/frequency design (rate per unit exposure, Poisson) means that an exposure offset is the correct treatment of exposure in the frequency model — [SynthETIC vignette](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/agi-lab/SynthETIC/master/SynthETIC/vignettes/SynthETIC-demo.Rmd) (read in full; applies by analogy).

### Inferences
- Frequency: with frequencies of 3-16% per exposure year and Poisson-type noise, most policy rows have zero claims, so the achievable D2 is small. The ceiling is set by the generator's true-rate variation across policies, which can be bounded empirically (not from literature): compute the D2 that the true rate would achieve under a Poisson/Tweedie draw, using the model's own fitted rates as a proxy, and compare to the leaderboard. Treat any gap beyond that as overfitting noise.
- Severity: with heavy-tailed peril-specific severities (including an 11.9M outlier on a mean of ~21k) the Tweedie deviance on the pure-premium target will be dominated by a few rows. Large-loss capping or a robust treatment is a modelling choice; whether the test set contains comparable outliers is unknowable.
- GLM versus GBM: if the generator is a multiplicative rating-factor table (typical in practice), a regularised log-link GLM with sensible binning should match or beat a GBM, which tracks your fold-A finding that the GLM beats the GBM. Shallow GBMs (depth 2-3, strong shrinkage) are the hedge for hidden interactions and non-linear risk scores. Evidence is only that your own result agrees; no cited source tests this on this data.
- Weather, address and customer tables: in a generator these are most plausibly (a) a latent exposure input (weather by address-year driving the peril frequency for home and perhaps motor) and (b) a way to create repeat structure (customers with several policies, addresses with several lines). Features that capture lagged or same-period weather at the address, and customer-level portfolio counts, are the natural candidates. Customer-history claim features need care: in a generator with no customer-level frailty they are noise; if a hidden customer score exists they carry real signal. This is testable on training folds (do prior-year customer claims predict the next year?).
- Exposure corruption on 2-3% of training rows suggests a deliberately injected data-quality defect; weight or drop those rows in training and check whether test exposure looks clean. Inference from the brief.

### Gaps
- No published estimate of D2 ceilings for synthetic multi-line insurance data; none found.
- No source comparing GLM and GBM on a synthetic generator with hidden risk scores matching this one.
- No information on how weather, address and customer tables enter any public generator.

## 4. Typical traps and clues in synthetic insurance data and how winners found them

### Takeaway
The only documented synthetic-insurance traps found are generator artefacts in SynthETIC (end-of-window pile-up, regime changes in settlement and claim size, size-dependent inflation) and the known data-quality issues in real freMTPL2. No write-up of a synthetic-data hackathon winner describing leakage detection was obtained.

### Cited Findings
- Regime changes are built into SynthETIC deliberately: a "legislative change" lowers settlement delays of small claims (from occurrence period 21) and cuts claim sizes by up to 40% for small claims after 20 quarters — [SynthETIC vignette](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/agi-lab/SynthETIC/master/SynthETIC/vignettes/SynthETIC-demo.Rmd) (read in full).
- Inflation is size-dependent (30% p.a. superimposed for the smallest claims, falling to zero at the reference claim and above), plus 2% base inflation, so a single global trend factor is misspecified in such generators — same vignette.
- Truncation artefact: payments beyond the maximum development period are bunched at the end of the window unless adjust = FALSE — same vignette. The authors recommend comparing plots with and without the adjustment to detect lag parameters that do not match the window.
- freMTPL2 (real data) has known quality problems: claim counts for policy IDs up to 24,500 in the frequency file lack matching severity records (one analysis takes counts from the severity file); policies with more than five claims look like errors (short exposures, one driver); exposure is commonly capped at one year; an older OpenML version was deactivated because of issues — [Search result summary of an arXiv analysis and OpenML](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2209.08030) and [OpenML 43593](https://api.openml.org/d/43593) (search snippets). These are real-data issues; they show the kind of orphan, duplicate and exposure inconsistencies to check for in any claims/policy split, not a statement about our data.
- The AIcrowd pricing game's first-place participant reportedly made few changes beyond adding claim-history features and cutting the profit loading by 1% — [r-bloggers / AIcrowd](https://www.r-bloggers.com/2020/12/insurance-pricing-game/) and [AIcrowd challenge](https://www.aicrowd.com/challenges/insurance-pricing-game) (search snippet, real not synthetic data).

### Inferences
- Checks suggested by the above, to run on our data: (1) compare report-delay and settlement-delay distributions by occurrence year to find regime changes and window artefacts (a pile-up at the maximum observable delay); (2) estimate severity trend per peril and per size band, not only globally; (3) look for orphans between claims and policy tables and duplicated claim rows; (4) test every non-rating column for dependence on claim outcomes (customer_service_contacts is already an example); (5) check whether exposure corruption is confined to training; (6) check whether the claim-year cutoff makes the latest training year incompletely developed (claims not yet reported), which would bias frequency downward for year 2024 relative to 2025 if the test target is complete. Point 6 is important for any reporting-delay generator and follows from the SynthETIC-style delay structure, but whether the 2025 target is on a fully developed or as-at-cutoff basis is not known from any source.
- The "declined by the insurer" rows and customer_service_contacts are probably deliberate probes of leakage and selection, since a synthetic data author controls what is generated after the claim. Inference.

### Gaps
- No write-up of a synthetic-insurance hackathon winner describing how leakage or regime changes were detected could be read (the Actuaries Institute article was blocked; AIcrowd winners used real data).
- No documentation of "duplicate/orphan" injection practices in any synthetic generator.

## 5. Emerging-market variants of such generators

### Takeaway
Nothing was found. No source describes emerging-market versions of insurance generators, their frequencies, perils or data-quality profile.

### Cited Findings
- The only related fact is that SynthETIC supports re-scaling to another currency/unit via a reference claim size and a configurable time unit, with defaults updating automatically — [SynthETIC vignette](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/agi-lab/SynthETIC/master/SynthETIC/vignettes/SynthETIC-demo.Rmd) (read in full). This shows how a generator author would port a developed-market calibration to a "novel market": by rescaling amounts and delays rather than building new structure.
- The IAA brief calls the task "a novel market" — [Kaggle API](https://www.kaggle.com/api/v1/competitions/list?search=IAA) (read in full).

### Inferences
- A "novel market" version of a generator would most plausibly reuse the same structure with a different parameter set: different perils (flood, theft, windstorm), higher reporting delays, larger share of open and repudiated claims, and more messy data. This is speculation; our own data (24-52% of cost on declined rows, corrupted exposure) is consistent with it but does not confirm it.

### Gaps
- No literature found on emerging-market synthetic insurance generators or their calibration; all five of the questions on frequencies, perils and data quality remain unanswered by sources.

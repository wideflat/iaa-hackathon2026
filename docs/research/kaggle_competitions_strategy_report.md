# Prove the level, pair the evidence, skip the tuning

**Bottom line.** The public record of similar competitions says the fastest way to lose a noisy, heavy-tailed, extrapolation-year contest is to optimise the wrong thing: the public leaderboard, a single validation window, or fine hyperparameters. What separated winners was (1) getting the overall prediction level right from an independent estimate, (2) validation built from several time-forward windows judged by mean and spread, (3) restraint in blending and post-processing, and (4) a few high-leverage design choices. Our own probes already show the level lever paid off (+0.0030 from trend and completeness). A rough quadratic fit to our three level probes implies the remaining level error costs only about 0.0004 per 5% miss, far less than the 0.0036 gap to the top public score. That gap is therefore more likely noise, tail handling or per-line detail than a level problem, and our unpaired fold CIs (about +-0.014) cannot tell which. Part 1 should be played as follows. Freeze the architecture. Spend about a day on three decision audits (per-line level, trend/completeness, tail cap), using paired and outlier-robust bootstrap on both folds. Fix the final-selection rule in writing before the last submissions. Put most remaining hours into the 60% of the score that is not the leaderboard, where the evidence is concrete and ours is strong. Part 2 should start from the Part 1 model as a documented prior, with an audit-first, offset-based, credibility-style adaptation and a pre-registered selection rule. Blending is deferred, and Section 6 sets out what would justify it.

**How to read this report.** Every recommendation carries a tag. **(S)** means source-backed: a source we read in full says it. **(A)** means adapted: a source supports a related practice and we translated it to our setting. **(I)** means inference: our reasoning, not a finding. Statements about our project (probe results, noise levels, incidents) are cited as project findings. Most external sources were seen only as search-result summaries because the sandbox blocked most hosts. Each source is marked as read or snippet where it matters. Items marked **[HUMAN CHECK]** must be verified against primary texts before anyone quotes them.

## 2. Model performance: level and tail decide the score, not the learner

**Level was the largest lever in the closest extrapolation-year contest.** In M5, Chris Deotte reported that a 5% total-level error moved the leaderboard by about 0.1 and could shift a rank by 200+ places; in all four prior years sales in the target window had exceeded the preceding window, so most teams over-predicted, and probing showed the true level was roughly 5% below that history ([Shake Up Explained](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163621)). Giba's best multiplier was about 1.03 on the public board and about 0.955 on private ([thread](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163047)). The way the 2nd-place team set the level is the instructive part: they aligned a per-store LightGBM to an independent aggregate forecast and called that alignment "most likely the most important trick" ([2nd place](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164599)). A per-item trend multiplier tuned on CV lost 0.14 on private versus 0.58797 without it ([aagapi](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163279)), and the 1st and 4th place teams used no multiplier at all ([1st](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684), [4th](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163216)). The lesson (S) is that the in-sample calibration step is safe, but the forward-looking extrapolation factor is the high-variance decision. Our pooled per-line severity trend and frequency completeness offset are exactly that kind of factor, and they were derived from the training data's own structure, not tuned on the leaderboard (project finding), which is the defensible version.

**Our remaining level error is probably small.** Project finding: the same predictions scaled x0.90, x1.00 and x1.10 scored 0.11554, 0.12153 and 0.12429. Fitting a quadratic through those three points (my arithmetic, three points, exact fit, no error bars) gives a public-optimal scale of about +13.5% when fitted in the scale (+16.4% when fitted in ln(scale); with three points the optimum is only pinned to roughly +13% to +16%) and a ceiling gain of about 0.0030 to 0.0032 over v1. That matches the +0.0030 trend-and-completeness gain, so the model now sits near the public optimum of that curve. The same curvature implies a 5% level miss costs only about 0.0004 and a 10% miss about 0.0016. The 0.0036 gap to the top public score would need a ~15% level miss to be explained by level alone (I). So further global level probing has little expected value, while per-line level, trend damping and the tail remain open. The notes' M5 2nd-place trick of averaging five multipliers adds no hedge when applied to one set of predictions, because the average of scaled copies is itself one scaled copy; the useful part of that approach is choosing the centre from an independent estimate (I).

**Tail handling is the most plausible unexplained gap.** The only documented Tweedie-aware winner, the Insurance Pricing Game 1st place, stacked seven base models (Tweedie GLM, LightGBM, XGBoost, CatBoost, DeepForest and two neural nets) with a Tweedie GLM meta-learner under 5-fold CV, and added a separate classifier for claims above 3k ([README](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/davidlkl/Insurance-Pricing-Game/main/README.md)). Allstate's 1st place trained extra models on percentile subsets of the loss "to get a better estimate of the exorbitant loss values" ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/allstate-claims-severity/discussion/26416)). A DataRobot handout reportedly capped the Fire Peril target at the 80th percentile of non-zero losses, but that is snippet-only and **[HUMAN CHECK]** against the [CAS handout](https://casact.org/sites/default/files/presentation/rpm_2015_handouts_paper_3896_handout_2468_0.pdf). Our capped Gamma plus per-line rebalancing is in the same family (A). What the sources do not establish is the right cap, or whether capped mass should be restored per line or per row; with one 11.9M claim in a 163k-claim set and a pooled metric, I expect the cap level and the excess load to be worth auditing before any new learner (I). The Denuit-Charpentier-Trufin autocalibration argument (snippet, [arXiv 2103.03635](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2103.03635)) explains why a regularised, deviance-minimising model can miss totals unless balanced, which is consistent with our earlier under-prediction (A).

**Architecture, features and seeds sit near the noise floor in every source.** Porto 3rd place wrote that "almost everything that you will try ... will result in no significant change" ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/porto-seguro-safe-driver-prediction/discussion/44608)). The Porto winner said two models scored 0.29502 and his six-model blend 0.2965, and that 32-bagging added little ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/porto-seguro-safe-driver-prediction/discussion/44629)). M5 1st place declined extra features because they raised CV standard deviation ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684)). Several Liberty Mutual Property Inspection entrants found plain XGBoost as good as blends ([example](https://www.kaggle.com/c/liberty-mutual-group-property-inspection-prediction/discussion/16254)). Our own results agree: hyperparameters, features, seeds and calibration curves moved validation by less than noise (project finding). On Tweedie power, M5 winners trained at 1.1 for very sparse unit counts, and no source compared powers on a scored holdout ([LightGBM docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/LightGBM/master/docs/Parameters.rst) give the default 1.5 and range [1,2)); our frequency x severity design already sidesteps the question, and a sweep would be noise chasing unless paired (I).

## 3. Validation and the final submission: decide the rule before the board decides it for you

**Use forward windows, mean and spread, and paired comparison.** M5 1st place chose models by the mean and standard deviation over three back-to-back windows plus the public window, "especially focusing on std" ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/163684)); 3rd place scored 14 past windows because WRMSSE fluctuated with the period ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/m5-forecasting-accuracy/discussion/164374)). A Porto entrant who rose from 1178th public to 29th private ran 5x5-fold CV with different fold seeds and 3 seeds per fold, ignored the public board, and used a positive-weight ridge blend on the same folds as level 1 ([post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/porto-seguro-safe-driver-prediction/discussion/44614)). Our two expanding-window folds (train 2021-22 predict 2023; train 2021-23 predict 2024) already follow the out-of-time principle. Three additions are supported. First, **report every candidate comparison as a paired difference on the same rows, clustered by customer, with an outlier-robust twin** (cap the per-row cost, drop the top-k claims, and show a leave-one-giant-claim-out table); our project finding that paired differences are about 10x tighter than unpaired is the main reason this is possible at all (A and I; no source gives paired Tweedie-D2 procedures). Second, **match claim maturity**: truncate validation-year claims to the development age the 2025 target will have, because unmatched maturity biases level more than ranking (I). Third, **treat any decision that flips on one claim as unresolved**, and decide it on prior plausibility and consistency across both folds (I). A third forward window (train 2021, predict 2022) is available but trained on one year, so I would show it as a robustness line, not a selection input (I).

**What the board can and cannot tell us.** The Roelofs et al. analysis of 120 Kaggle competitions (snippet) found little evidence of adaptive overfitting and attributed big shake-ups mainly to crowded rankings and distribution shift ([paper page](https://papers.nips.cc/paper/9117-a-meta-analysis-of-overfitting-in-machine-learning)); an AIcrowd simulation found scores on random test subsets of similar size differing by up to 10% ([post](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/simulating-leaderboard-shakeups-to-give-some-context/5917)). Porto, Allstate and Liberty Fire Peril all record large public-to-private moves for high-variance components (for example Fire Peril neural and linear models fell from 0.38 to 0.26 and 0.28 private while the best XGBoost fell to 0.31; [post](https://www.kaggle.com/c/liberty-mutual-fire-peril/discussion/10194)). Our top-5 position is inside a crowded range, and with about 33% of rows public and a pooled metric dominated by a few large claims, the effective sample size is closer to the number of large claims in the public slice than to the row count (I). **A cheap simulation would convert this from worry to a number**: resample 33% row subsets of fold-B predictions and measure the standard deviation of D2 for one model and, more usefully, of the difference between our model and a variant (I). If the standard deviation of the difference exceeds 0.0036, the top team's lead is not evidence of a better model.

**Probing is a narrow tool, and the rules need a human read.** The notes found no source in which winners calibrate level against the public board of a Tweedie-type metric; probing in the literature is mostly leakage exploitation, and some hosts ban it outright, for example ADDI ([thread](https://discourse.aicrowd.com/t/welcome-to-the-addi-alzheimers-detection-challenge/3896/15)). **[HUMAN CHECK]** the Kaggle rules for any language on probing before further level probes. The Ladder paper shows repeated submissions can overfit the holdout, with a bound growing roughly with the square root of queries over rows (snippet; [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1502.04585)). Probing a single scale parameter uses few degrees of freedom, so the adaptive risk is small next to sampling noise, but the public-optimal scale is set by the largest claims in that 33% slice (I). The practical policy (I): at most one more level probe pair on the final candidate; move the level only if the CV-implied and public-implied directions agree; and use a precision-weighted average of the two estimates, with variances from the cluster bootstrap. Everything else is decided by CV alone.

**One final submission means hedging inside the model, not across entries.** Allstate and Porto participants reported that the best-CV entry beat the best-LB entry ([Allstate](https://www.kaggle.com/c/allstate-claims-severity/discussion/26411), [Porto 18th](https://www.kaggle.com/c/porto-seguro-safe-driver-prediction/discussion/44579)), but they could submit two. We cannot. The rule I recommend (A, from those posts and the notes' synthesis): write down before the last day that the final model is the candidate with the best pooled, outlier-robust CV across both folds; ties inside the paired CI go to the simpler, more regularised candidate; the public score serves only to veto a candidate that is clearly worse outside its noise band or to adjust level as above. Log CV and public score for every submission and plot the rank correlation. The 10 daily submissions are measurement capacity, not a climbing budget. Freeze changes about 12 hours before the deadline except for bug fixes.

**Leakage is the one place the board is decisive.** A column generated by claims in training gave D2 0.55 against a leaderboard near 0.12 (project finding). That gap is the reason the public score should stay in the workflow as an alarm even when it is useless for ranking. Kaggle's own judging method looks for "shortcuts and tomfoolery" end to end ([skill, read in full](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kaggle/kaggle-skills/main/hackathon-judging/SKILL.md)), so the incident, the guard test and the D2 > 0.35 alarm are both defence and evidence.

## 4. The 60% outside the score: make verification visible and checkable

**What the graders are likely to be.** The host skill Kaggle publishes (read in full) describes pulling every writeup, linked artefact and video into a bundle; preferring binary criteria and pairwise LLM comparison with Bradley-Terry or ELO scoring; requiring graders to be validated against humans and defended against prompt injection; and sending top submissions to manual review ([skill](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/kaggle/kaggle-skills/main/hackathon-judging/SKILL.md)). The IAA's own 2025 hackathon (snippets) used a three-stage process (AI screen, AI plus human ranking, human judging) and named winners for interpretability and "strongest holistic GenAI enhanced workflow", with the third-place entry noted for one of the best predictive models ([Jan 2026 brief](https://actuaries.org/news-post/news-brief-january-21-2026/); criteria in the [Nov 2025 brief](https://actuaries.org/news-post/news-brief-november-19-2025/)). It is an inference, not a finding, that 2026 reuses that pipeline (I). If it does, the artefacts must serve an LLM that extracts evidence and a human who skims the first page.

**Structure for 25% (effective, verified AI use) and 20% (trust, validation, guardrails).** The notes found no winner artefacts, so the following is an adaptation of regulated-domain practice, not a copy of anything that won. Show verification as artefacts: a verification matrix (module, test, independent oracle, reviewer, date), where the independent oracle for the metric is already sklearn agreement to 1e-9 (project finding); a ledger of AI errors caught, for example the helper-session claim of several-fold recovery bias re-tested and corrected to about 1.5%, plus the leakage catch, the Gamma failure on zero-cost claims, the cache race and the OOM incident (project findings); helper-session controls (no commit or submit rights, baselines reproduced exactly at 0.1320 GBM and 0.1399 GLM before results are counted); and an escalation ladder from automated guard to stop, triage, decision-record entry and regression test (A and I; derived from SR 11-7 "effective challenge", which is a snippet-level source). The team's own `AI_USAGE.md` still ends with a blank line for who reviewed which decision and when (project finding). Fill it with names and dates and give the reviewer authority to block a submission; **this is the cheapest high-signal fix in the whole plan** (I). Reporting negative and unresolved results (GLM versus GBM tied after capping; hyperparameters below noise) is part of verified use and directly counters the "claims without uncertainty" failure mode (I).

**Standards: cite few, and only after a human has read them.** The notes' consensus is a small mapping table (standard, paraphrased requirement, where it is evidenced in the repo) with an explicit statement that the hackathon is not a signed actuarial opinion. Candidates: ASOP 56 (modelling, including reliance on models built by others), ASOP 23 (data quality and disclosure of limitations), ASOP 41 (communications), the American Academy's 2024 professionalism paper on generative AI (not binding; says GenAI is a model, so ASOP 56 applies; [page](https://actuary.org/article/actuarial-professionalism-considerations-for-generative-ai/)), FRC TAS 100 guidance and SR 11-7. Everything about them was seen at snippet level. **[HUMAN CHECK]** every section number, every quotation, the claim (from a third-party reviewer) that ASOP 41 requires disclosure of AI reliance, and the EU AI Act application dates, which conflict across secondary sources. The notes warn that a hallucinated citation is exactly the failure this rubric penalises; one snippet-level study found 18-55% of model-generated citations fabricated depending on model ([PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10484980/)). Rule: citation details must be pasted from the primary page by a named person, with a verified-on date, and anything unverified is omitted.

**Communication (15%) rests on the first 5 pages and 5 minutes.** Only that material is graded (team overview notes). The notes' proposal, which is inference, is a self-contained page 1 with the result and its uncertainty, a rubric-to-evidence table with file and test links, and the leakage catch as the headline story; pages 2 to 5 follow the rubric names (data and ASOP 23-style framing, modelling and adaptation, AI use and verification, trust and limits). The video mirrors this with a clean-checkout run, the leak catch shown red then green, and an AI-error example. Keep claims machine-checkable (a script comparing report numbers with log rows), keep the repo structure predictable, license the code, and keep data out of the repo. **Do not write instructions to the grader** such as requests for high scores; the host skill treats prompt injection and "tomfoolery" as things to detect (read in full). Pick one headline number with its bootstrap interval and avoid stale figures across README, report and logs.

## 5. Part 2: treat the Part 1 model as a prior and the audit as the product

Nothing in the notes describes the Part 2 dataset or rules, so every statement here is conditional. What the organisers asked for, per the notes, is to identify what changed, respond, and communicate remaining uncertainty. The notes' structure for that story is a change log with evidence, size of impact, action, residual uncertainty and owner, plus a list of items checked and found stable; no published template exists, so this is design (I).

**Detect first, with outcome-side checks that covariate tests cannot do.** Schema, unit and coding audit; missingness including pseudo-missing values; level overlap against Part 1; PSI, KS and chi-square per feature reported with the share of exposure affected; a domain classifier with grouped CV (adversarial validation; snippets, for example [KDnuggets](https://www.kdnuggets.com/2016/10/adversarial-validation-explained.html)); then frequency per peril with CIs, severity quantiles and cap-hit rate, report-lag CDFs, and exposure conventions (all I). The decisive habit from our own record is to **run the leak and outcome-dependence checks on every new column on day one**: the Part 1 trap was a column generated by claims in training that is only baseline in test, and a data author with a new market has every reason to hide a similar one (I). Two further hypotheses worth checking because the SynthETIC generator, the only one read in full, has them: severity inflation that depends on claim size, so a single pooled per-line trend may be misspecified, and regime changes by occurrence period ([vignette](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/agi-lab/SynthETIC/master/SynthETIC/vignettes/SynthETIC-demo.Rmd)). Whether the IAA generator has either is unknown (A). The Argentine motor study (IAA 2017 presentation, snippet) found bodily injury and damage claims inflating differently by claim type and legal route ([slides](https://actuaries.org/app/uploads/2025/07/Cuypers_PPT_Panama2017.pdf)), which is the argument for per-peril trend checks in a new market; our Part 1 pooled-per-line trend should be re-estimated, not carried over (A).

**Respond with a ladder, cheapest first, and let validation decide.** The ladder from the notes (all I unless a tool fact is cited): baseline 0 is the Part 1 model as-is plus a per-peril level correction; baseline 1 is recalibration on the link scale with an intercept and slope per peril; then a shallow, heavily regularised GBM correction on top of the Part 1 prediction with a zero-tree option; then a credibility-weighted blend of the adapted model and a model fitted only on Part 2; then a scratch GLM. The tooling facts are source-backed from documentation read in full: LightGBM accepts per-row `init_score` on the dataset and `init_model` for continued training, with `init_score` overridden if `init_model` is passed; `Booster.refit` with `decay_rate` blends new and old leaf values, a literal shrink-toward-prior operation; `path_smooth` shrinks small nodes toward their parent; `monotone_constraints` exist; and XGBoost's `base_margin` is documented as the GLM-offset analogue ([LightGBM basic.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/LightGBM/master/python-package/lightgbm/basic.py), [Parameters](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/microsoft/LightGBM/master/docs/Parameters.rst), [XGBoost intercept docs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/dmlc/xgboost/master/doc/tutorials/intercept.rst)) (S for the tool behaviour, A for using it this way). The interaction of `boost_from_average` with an `init_score` under a Tweedie or Poisson objective was not tested and needs a five-minute check (I). The one insurance paper on this idea, Dutang, Spedicato and Guibert (Variance, 2025), reports that pretraining on a broad portfolio and adapting to a small book beat training on the small book alone and was competitive with Buhlmann-Straub credibility; that is an abstract-level result **[HUMAN CHECK]** ([CAS abstract](https://www.casact.org/abstract/adjusting-manual-rates-own-experience-comparing-credibility-approach-machine-learning)). A separate snippet found TabPFN did not consistently beat established baselines for motor pricing, so a foundation-model shortcut is not supported.

**Carry the M5 lesson across: extrapolation factors are the dangerous part.** With only 2 to 4 years in a new market, a trend or completeness factor estimated on Part 2 has wide uncertainty. Treat them as scenario parameters with a tornado table (trend plus or minus a few points, slow-reporting scenario, exposure convention, cap level, Tweedie power) and choose the centre from an independent estimate, which is the M5 2nd-place logic (A). The notes' pre-registered selection rule is also useful (A and I): best mean forward D2 within one standard error, then the simplest candidate, with a locked last fold for a single check. Because GLM and GBM tied on paired Part 1 comparison and the new market gives less data, it would not be surprising if the scratch GLM or the zero-tree prior wins in Part 2; the plan should accept that outcome without drama (I). **Unknown and must be checked by a human**: whether the rules allow Part 1 data or Part 1 predictions in Part 2, how long the Part 2 window is, and which metric and power Part 2 uses.

**What evidence would justify the blend.** Blending is deferred at the team's request and I agree it is lower priority than items 2 to 5 and 7 to 9. Reopen it only if all of the following hold: the paired cluster bootstrap on the same rows shows blend minus best single model with an interval that excludes zero on both folds, and the sign survives dropping the top-k claims; the per-row loss differences between the GLM and the GBM are not highly correlated, so there is something to average; and the blend's gain exceeds the seed-to-seed standard deviation. Use equal or non-negative weights on the same folds at both levels, and never a GBM stacker (S: Porto 1st, 2nd and 29th-private, Allstate 3rd). The gains reported in the sources are of the same size as our unresolved differences (Porto +0.0015 on 0.295; Allstate 1105 to 1098 on MAE; Rohlik 2.3%), so the real justification may be risk reduction on the unseen 67% rather than a measurable gain, and that would have to be written as a judgement, not a result (I).

## Conclusion

The competition record does not show that better learners win noisy heavy-tailed contests; it shows that the winners reduced the variance of their decisions. Our three level probes make that concrete: most of the available level gain has already been taken, so the next real differences are in how we justify the per-line level, the trend, the cap and the final pick, not in the model class. The most useful new work is therefore a noise calibration of the public board and a written, paired, outlier-robust decision procedure that doubles as governance evidence.

The same artefacts serve the 60% of the score that is not the leaderboard, which is where our assets (leak guards, decision record, caught-error logs, helper-session controls) are strongest and least exploited. Part 2 benefits from the same discipline: arrive with a prior, an audit and a selection rule fixed in advance, and treat the new market's extrapolation factors as scenarios with ranges, because M5 shows that those factors, not the learner, are where the ranking is won and lost. The caveat for all of it is that these are conclusions from other tasks, scored in other metrics, read mostly through summaries, and our own first measurement on this task should override any of them.
